Ganba: the algorithm gacha

A gacha where the cards are algorithms, rarity is complexity, and fusion is composition — wrapped around the one question every loot box quietly poses: how many pulls to collect them all?

This started as a Java data-structures homework (Ganba Infusion, CMSI) — a gacha for collecting CS professors, built to exercise a from-scratch dynamic array. Here it is repackaged: the collectibles are algorithms, and underneath the pulls is the coupon-collector problem, a live Monte Carlo lab, and the economics of pity. See origin & provenance.

3 · The math of collecting them all

Collecting every distinct card is the coupon-collector problem. With equal drop rates over $n$ cards the expected number of pulls is the clean $n\,H_n=n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tfrac1k$. With unequal rarities it generalizes to an integral over the per-card probabilities $p_i$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{pulls to complete}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}\big(1-e^{-p_i t}\big)\Big)\,dt$$

5 · Pity & the economics of the box

Real gacha systems don't leave the rarest cards to pure chance — they add pity: a guaranteed rare-or-better at a hard streak limit, and often a soft pity ramp where the odds climb as you go dry. Pity trades a fatter average cost for a much shorter tail — it caps the catastrophe of the unlucky player. That tail is exactly what regulators and the psychology literature focus on: the worst-case spend, not the average one.

6 · Origin & provenance

The mechanic is a port of Ganba Infusion, my CMSI data-structures homework: a gacha for collecting three CS professors — Edmiston (rarity 3), B.J. (4), and Forney (5, the reinforcement-learning professor) — at 60/30/10 odds, built on a hand-written dynamic array with pull, fusion, sell, and an aidStudy loop. Flip the professor homage skin toggle in section 1 to collect the original cast.

What's new here is the lens. The pull loop is unchanged in spirit; the additions are the coupon-collector analysis, the Monte Carlo convergence, and the pity economics. The algorithm roster and its complexity tiers are my curation, drawn from coursework I've worked through (Stanford CS 161, LMU data structures) — Karatsuba, Karger's randomized min-cut, union-find, Dijkstra, the sorts. Monte-Carlo-vs-theory is the same move as my robot-arm crawler writeup.
