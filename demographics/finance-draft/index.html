Finance: the productivity-weighted age structure

Scott Nelson · Data Engineer · November 2026 · a proposed section for The Shape of People to Come

The demographics essay already draws a life-cycle of labor income and consumption (Figure 3) and scores every country on an economic support ratio built from those same curves. This draft takes a simpler claim, states it as a number, and tests whether the number sorts countries in a useful way. It is meant to sit beside Figure 3, not replace it. It is not yet on the geopolitical atlas.

1 · The claim

For any one person, labor income (and with it, income-tax contribution) is low at labor-market entry, rises through working life, peaks in middle age or toward the end of a working career, and falls in retirement. Dependents sit at both ends of the same life: children, then retirees. If that age profile is used as a weight on a country's population by age, the result is a single score: how much of the high-earning part of the life-cycle the country's current age structure is sitting on. Call it the productivity-weighted age structure, \(S\).

The score can be computed at today's age structure and, with the UN medium-variant projection, at 2050. The question for this draft is whether the ranking, and especially the change between those two dates, is informative: whether it separates countries that are in the middle of a working-age bulge from countries that are still very young, and from countries that have already aged past the bulge.

2 · The weight

The weight \(w(a)\) is a measured labor-income age profile, not a freehand sketch. It is the unweighted average of 56 country-team profiles from the National Transfer Accounts project, published with Mason, Lee, Abrigo and Lee's 2017 UN technical paper. Each country's profile is normalized so the mean labor income of ages 30 to 49 equals 1, which is the NTA convention. The average peaks at age 44, at 1.05. It is essentially zero before age 15, about 0.36 at 65, and about 0.07 at 80.

The shape is not universal. In the same 56-country file, labor income peaks at 29 in Vietnam and 32 in China (both mid-2000s vintages), and at 49 in the United States and 47 in Japan. A single global average is a choice: it isolates the effect of age structure from the effect of each country's labor market. The cost of that choice is listed with the other caveats below. The faint gold line is the stylized curve already used for Figure 3 on the live page, renormalized to the same 30-to-49 mean so the two can be compared. They are close; country scores computed from either have a correlation of 0.99.

3 · The score

For a country at date \(t\), with population \(P(a,t)\) at age \(a\) and population share \(s(a,t) = P(a,t) / \sum_{a'} P(a',t)\),

\[ S_t \;=\; \sum_a w(a)\, s(a,t) \]

This is a weighted sum: the average labor-income weight of a person drawn at random from that population. A country made entirely of 30-to-49-year-olds would score 1. The world, population-weighted across the 236 countries and territories in the essay's data, scores 0.50 in 2025. The units are prime-age labor-income equivalents per person.

A weighted product of the same terms is a poor fit, and is not used. Labor income is zero, or as good as zero, in childhood, so any product (or geometric mean) that includes children collapses to zero in every country. A product of the population shares themselves, raised to the weights, also changes when the width of the age bins changes, which is arbitrary. The arithmetic sum has neither problem, and it is already the numerator of the National Transfer Accounts economic support ratio:

\[ SR_t \;=\; \frac{\sum_a y_l(a)\, P(a,t)}{\sum_a c(a)\, P(a,t)} \]

where \(c(a)\) is the matching average consumption profile. \(S\) divides effective producers by headcount; \(SR\) divides them by effective consumers. With one global profile the two rankings almost agree (correlation 0.99 across 236 countries in 2025), because consumption varies less by age than labor income does. \(SR\) is slightly harder on already-aged countries, whose older members consume more than children do. Both disagree with the ordinary headcount ratio of workers to retirees, which ignores children entirely. That is the Nigeria result already in the essay: a high headcount ratio, a low weighted score.

Population counts are UN World Population Prospects 2024, medium variant, in 5-year age groups, taken from the data already shipped with the essay. The 2050 figures use the same medium-variant projection. Weights inside each 5-year group are a simple average of the five single-year values. Recomputing the essay's economic support ratio this way, against that page's official single-year figure, differs by 0.001 on average, so the binning approximation is small. Levels of \(SR\) here use the NTA average profiles, each normalized to its own 30-to-49 mean; those levels are profile-relative and should not be compared with the essay's published support-ratio levels, which use a same-scale stylized pair. Ranks and changes are the reliable part.

4 · What the ranking shows

The chart below is a test set of 34 countries: major economies, plus a few young and already-aged contrasts. The full 236-country list sits behind the scatter that follows. A 2025-only or 2050-only bar chart is one click away; the default is a dumbbell that shows both dates at once.

Among these 34, the highest 2025 scores belong to countries whose adults are stacked in the high-weight ages and who have few children: Saudi Arabia (0.580), South Korea (0.579), China (0.569), Iran (0.562) and Thailand (0.561). The lowest belong to the youngest countries, where close to half the population is under 15: Niger (0.331), Nigeria (0.380), Ethiopia (0.391), Pakistan (0.413) and Kenya (0.415). Japan, the oldest large country, scores 0.495, below the United States at 0.515 and just below the world average of 0.500.

The full 236-country list is led by Gulf states with large working-age migrant populations: Qatar at 0.713, the United Arab Emirates at 0.677, Kuwait at 0.665. That is a real feature of their age structure, not a data error, and it is also a reminder that \(S\) is not GDP and is not a measure of output per worker. The bottom of the full list is young Central and West Africa: the Central African Republic at 0.297, then Niger, Somalia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Mali and Chad.

The more informative view is the change to 2050. In this set, South Korea has the sharpest fall, from 0.579 (17th of 236) to 0.466 (188th), as today's 45-to-64 cohort moves into the low-weight retirement ages. Taiwan, not in the chart but in the full file, falls almost as far. Spain, Italy, Poland, Thailand and China follow. India moves the other way, from 0.518 (101st) to 0.542 (19th). The Philippines rises from 0.493 (135th) to 0.556 (10th). Bangladesh, Egypt, Mexico and Indonesia also gain. The younger African countries rise the most in raw points (Nigeria +0.089, Niger +0.102, Uganda +0.113) but start so low that they are still below the world average in 2050. The United States changes little (0.515 to 0.499).

The world average barely moves
The population-weighted world average of \(S\) is 0.500 in 2025 and 0.501 in 2050. Aging in East Asia and Europe is offset, in the global mean, by large cohorts entering working age in South Asia and Africa. What changes is who holds the favorable age structure, not the world's average score.

Plotted against median age, \(S\) has the shape the claim predicts: low in the youngest countries, highest around a median age of 30 to 40, and lower again past 50. The relationship is not a straight line. A linear correlation of 0.74 understates the bend. A simpler statistic, the share of the population aged 45 to 64, tracks \(S\) only loosely (correlation 0.77). The full curve adds the gradual rise through the late twenties and thirties and the fade after 65, and it counts children at nearly zero.

5 · Age structure under the curve

The six panels below put the weight curve on top of each country's population shares, in 2025 and in 2050. Nigeria still has a wide child base in 2050, so its rising score has further to go. India and Mexico fill in the high-weight ages. The United States barely changes shape. South Korea's 2025 pile in the late working ages becomes, by 2050, a pile at 70 and older, which is why its score is the steepest fall in the set. Japan is already there.

6 · Caveats

Four limits sit on every number above.

First, the same age profile is applied to every country. Real profiles differ in peak age, in how long labor income lasts past 60, and in how much work is informal. Holding the profile fixed is what makes \(S\) a pure age-structure score. Using each country's own NTA profile, where one exists, would mix labor-market institutions into the ranking. That is a useful sensitivity, and it is not in this draft.

Second, the 56 measured profiles are vintages from 2002 to 2015. China's 2007 profile, which peaks at 32, may not describe China in 2025. The average is a historical cross-section, not a 2025 measurement.

Third, \(S\) is not GDP, not income per capita, and not a forecast of growth. It does not know about capital, education, labor-force participation, hours, or wages. The first demographic dividend in the NTA literature is the growth rate of the economic support ratio, not the level of \(S\). A high score says the age structure is favorable for labor income under a common profile. It does not say the country is rich, well governed, or about to grow.

Fourth, 2050 figures are the UN medium variant. Everyone who will be over 25 in 2050 is already alive, so the productive ages in 2050 are mostly a survival-and-aging calculation. Fertility and migration still move the child shares and, in the Gulf and in a few other countries, the working-age counts.

7 · Sources and methods

Population. United Nations, Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division, World Population Prospects 2024, medium variant, 5-year age groups, taken from the country files already shipped with The Shape of People to Come (built by scripts/build_demographics_data.py from the PPgp/wpp2024 package). © 2024 United Nations, licensed CC BY 3.0 IGO.

Labor-income and consumption profiles. Andrew Mason, Ronald Lee, Michael Abrigo and Sang-Hyop Lee, "Support Ratio and Demographic Dividends: Estimates for the World," UN DESA Population Division Technical Paper 2017/1. The 56 country-team ("Country estimate") profiles were downloaded from the NTA project as Yl and C CSV. Project page: UNPD Project 2016. Each profile is already normalized so the mean of ages 30 to 49 equals 1. The global weight is the unweighted average of those 56, then renormalized so the convention holds exactly. NTA profiles end at age 90; ages 91 to 100+ reuse the age-90 value.

Method. National Transfer Accounts (Lee and Mason; UN DESA NTA Manual, 2013). The economic support ratio and the first and second demographic dividends: Mason, Lee, Abrigo and Lee 2017; Lee and Mason 2006, 2010, 2011. The first dividend is the growth rate of \(SR\), not the level of \(S\). The essay's Figure 3 and its support-ratio discussion are the parent of this section. Computation is in scripts/compute_scores.py in this draft folder, and is reproducible from the shipped WPP files plus the downloaded NTA CSV.

Charts: Plotly.js 2.35.2. Math: KaTeX 0.16.9. In this review copy both load from pinned CDN URLs with subresource-integrity hashes; the live essay self-hosts them. This is a review draft on a branch, not part of the live site.
