# Mises Weighs Promises, Not Population Pyramids

Four arguments in Ludwig von Mises's *The Theory of Money and Credit* connect accurately to the economics of ageing, and none of them is about ageing: **the words pension, old age, widow, orphan and fixed income never occur in the book**, and population appears only as a source of demand for money. The strongest connection is his account of inflation as the finance of a government that "cannot negotiate loans and dare not levy taxes" (¶ II.13.18, written in 1924), joined to his 1952 remark that the common man "himself is a creditor" (¶ IV.21.17). The second is his 1912 separation of a fall in the natural rate of interest from a money rate pushed below it, which files the low interest of a high-saving old society under the first heading and keeps it out of his crisis theory. The third is his 1924 warning that reserves held as claims on a centre country depend on "the behavior of one government, namely, that of the United States" (¶ III.20.42). The fourth, pay-as-you-go pensions as claims on future workers, rests in this book on nothing more than his definition of a claim (¶ I.3.9); the sentence that makes the point outright, that a social-security IOU "is a check drawn upon the future taxpayers", is in *Human Action* (1949). Each connection meets serious counter-evidence: Japan's fifteen years of falling prices and the price-indexation of most public pensions, Mises's own 1949 reversal on saving and interest, valuation transfers that have run toward creditor nations as well as away from them, and the Samuelson–Aaron result that an unfunded scheme earns the growth of the wage bill. The demography itself (workers per retiree, life-cycle saving, estimates of the natural rate, reserve shares) belongs entirely to later writers, and every bridge from Mises's categories to cohorts is the essayist's own and has to be labelled so.

## The book never mentions old age, and its usable passages carry three dates

The first constraint is a silence. A case-insensitive search of all four Parts, the prefaces and the appendices of the Liberty Fund text hosted by Econlib returns no occurrence of pension (the only raw hits sit inside "suspension"), pensioner, widow, orphan, old age, fixed income, elderly, thrift, children or any word in demograph-; "annuities" occurs once, as a bank asset (Part Three, ch. 18 §5, ¶ III.18.31), and "insurance policies" once, in the 1952 list of what creditors hold (¶ IV.21.17) ([Econlib edition](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html)). Population enters the monetary theory in one way only: more people demand more money. "Decrease of population and retrogression of the exchange economy, bring about a contraction in it" (Part Three, ch. 17 §2, ¶ III.17.7) is the nearest the book comes to a shrinking society, and its subject is cash balances, not saving or interest ([Econlib ch. 17](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=21)). Where Part Four mentions population it assumes growth: the market economy "provides ample sustenance for a steadily increasing population" (Part Four, ch. 21 §1, ¶ IV.21.2). Saving is tied to income, never to age: "individuals with large incomes make better provision for the future than individuals with small incomes" (Part Three, ch. 19 §2, ¶ III.19.15), a regularity Mises takes from Irving Fisher ([Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)). **Any sentence that has this book speaking of pensioners, cohorts or age structure is the essayist's inference.**

The second constraint is dating. The English text of Parts One to Three is H. E. Batson's 1934 translation of the second German edition of 1924; the 1912 first edition was never translated into English, and what is 1912 can be fixed only through Jörg Guido Hülsmann's collation of the two German editions and Mises's own 1924 preface ([Hülsmann 2012, pp. 2, 23–24](https://cdn.mises.org/Theory%20of%20Money%20and%20Fiduciary%20Media.pdf); [Econlib prefaces, ¶ HP.25](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4)). On that evidence the welfare thesis (¶ I.5.18), the natural-rate argument and the cycle mechanism (ch. 19 §§1–4) and the world capital market (ch. 20 §§3–4) are 1912 text; so are the two kinds of credit (ch. 15), though there the dating rests only on the absence of any post-1912 reference. The whole "Inflationism" section with its fiscal and political motives (¶¶ II.13.8–35), the argument that anticipated depreciation is priced into interest (¶¶ II.12.13–14), chapter 14, the two-paragraph crisis conclusion (¶¶ III.19.44–45) and the critique of the gold-exchange standard (¶¶ III.20.41–45) are 1924 text, written after the Austrian and German hyperinflations ([Hülsmann 2012, pp. 25–29](https://cdn.mises.org/Theory%20of%20Money%20and%20Fiduciary%20Media.pdf); [Hülsmann 2007, p. 254](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf); [Econlib ch. 20, translator's note to §9](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)). Part Four was written in 1952 and first published in the 1953 Yale edition, and the prefaces are signed June 1934, March 1924 and June 1952 ([Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25); [Econlib prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4)). **"In 1912 Mises argued that governments inflate because they dare not tax" would misdate the sentence by twelve years and one hyperinflation.**

Mises did write about pensions, but in other books. *Human Action* (1949) adds the missing item to the creditor's list, "in this age of bonds and debentures, mortgage banks, saving banks, life insurance policies, and social security benefits, the masses of people with more moderate income are rather themselves creditors" (Part Four, ch. XX §2, ¶ 4.XX.12), and treats a social-security entitlement as a claim on future taxpayers in a passage quoted in the next section ([Econlib, *Human Action* ch. XX](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/HmA/msHmA.html?chapter_num=23)). A 1950 newspaper article, "Economic Aspects of the Pension Problem", concludes that under a depreciating dollar "The pensions they will be entitled one day to claim will be a mere sham" ([*Planning for Freedom*, p. 89](https://cdn.mises.org/Planning%20for%20Freedom%20and%20Twelve%20other%20Essays%20and%20Addresses.pdf)). Later Austrians added institutional arguments: Jesús Huerta de Soto proposed using the assets freed by a move to full-reserve banking to retire public debt or finance a transition away from pay-as-you-go ([Huerta de Soto, pp. 197, 791–797](https://cdn.mises.org/Money_Bank_Credit_and_Economic_Cycles_De%20Soto.pdf)), and Hülsmann argued that fiat inflation makes it "completely pointless in our day to hoard dollar or euro notes to prepare for retirement" ([Hülsmann 2008, p. 183](https://cdn.mises.org/The%20Ethics%20of%20Money%20Production_2.pdf)). No published application of the natural-rate concept or the cycle theory to pension schemes or to age structure was found, a negative result that rests on one search and on the bibliographies of the works read. Those bridges would be the essay's own.

The modern evidence used below comes in two grades. The Mises texts, Hülsmann, the BIS papers and the Wikipedia pages were read directly and their quotations checked. Figures from the OECD, IMF, NBER, national statistical offices, central banks other than the BIS and journal abstracts were captured from search extracts because those hosts were unreachable; they are paraphrased here, never quoted, and each needs a page-level check before the essay prints a number.

## A pension entitlement fits Mises's definition of a claim, but he applied it only in 1949

**What Mises wrote.** The book's vocabulary for a promise is exact, and it separates the promise from the goods.

> "Claims are not goods; they are means of obtaining disposal over goods. This determines their whole nature and economic significance. They themselves are not valued directly, but indirectly; their value is derived from that of the economic goods to which they refer. Two elements are involved in the valuation of a claim: first, the value of the goods to whose possession it gives a right; and, second, the greater or less probability that possession of the goods in question will actually be obtained. Furthermore, if the claim is to come into force only after a period of time, then consideration of this circumstance will constitute a third factor in its valuation."
>
> Part One, ch. 3 "The Various Kinds of Money", §1 "Money and Money Substitutes", ¶ I.3.9 ([Econlib ch. 3](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=7))

Further sentences complete the real-resources half of the argument. "An increase in the quantity of money can no more increase the welfare of the members of a community, than a diminution of it can decrease their welfare" (Part One, ch. 5 §1, ¶ I.5.18; 1912), and between countries as between persons "goods and services can only be paid for with other goods and services" (Part Two, ch. 10 §2, ¶ II.10.11) ([Econlib ch. 5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9); [ch. 10](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=14)). Part Three sorts credit by whether anyone gives something up: "A credit transaction is an exchange of present goods for future goods" (ch. 15 §3, ¶ III.15.9), whereas in circulation credit "the loans are granted out of a fund that did not exist before the loans were granted" (ch. 15 §4, ¶ III.15.23), and Part Three's best-known sentence follows from the distinction: "The banks cannot evoke capital out of nothing" (ch. 17 §6, ¶ III.17.30) ([Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19); [ch. 17](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=21)). The 1952 text restates the constraint for a state at war in words that apply by analogy to any large public commitment: "Whether there is inflation or not, the material equipment required by the armed forces must be provided out of the available means by restricting consumption for nonvital purposes, by intensifying production in order to increase output, and by consuming a part of the capital previously accumulated" (Part Four, ch. 21 §4, ¶ IV.21.44) ([Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25)). Mises never turns any of these sentences on a pension. He also guards a distinction the essay must not blur: money itself is not a claim in his scheme ("Money is always an economic good", ch. 5 §1, ¶ I.5.17), and his tempting bread-ticket sentence, that "it is impossible to satisfy an increase in the demand, say, for bread by issuing more breadtickets without adding to the actual supply of bread itself" (ch. 3 §1, ¶ I.3.12), is reported as an argument about what is special about money, since more claims can satisfy a greater demand for money.

**What Mises wrote elsewhere, and what later writers added.** The explicit statement is in *Human Action*, where "Paul in the year 1940" pays a hundred dollars into social security: "The government's IOU is a check drawn upon the future taxpayers. In 1970 a certain Peter may have to fulfill the government's promise although he himself does not derive any benefit from the fact that Paul in 1940 saved one hundred dollars" (Part Six, ch. XXXV §3, ¶ 6.XXXV.53), glossed in the next paragraph as "It is the Peters of 1970 who owe it to the Pauls of 1940" (¶ 6.XXXV.54) ([Econlib, *Human Action* ch. XXXV](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/HmA/msHmA.html?chapter_num=38)). The arithmetic is not his at all. A pay-as-you-go scheme with a fixed contribution rate pays each cohort the growth of the contribution base: population growth in Samuelson's 1958 consumption-loan model ([Samuelson 1958](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/258100)), population growth plus real-wage growth in Aaron's 1966 version, whose condition is that "social insurance can increase the welfare of each person if the sum of the rates of growth of population and real wages exceeds the rate of interest" (Aaron's words as quoted in [Drees 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00909)). The arithmetic of the essay's pension card, in which the contribution rate that holds benefits constant rises as workers per retiree fall, descends from that literature. So does the measurement of the stock: accrued-to-date social-insurance pension entitlements were about **434% of GDP in Italy** for 2021 ([Istat](https://www.istat.it/it/files//2024/03/Nota-informativa-trattamenti-pensionistici-14032024-EN.pdf)), and the 2026 United States Trustees put the open-group unfunded obligation at **$29.3 trillion** through 2100 ([SSA](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/TR/2026/II_A_highlights.html)); the first is a closed-group stock and the second a net shortfall, so the two must never be set side by side.

**What would be the essay's own.** Three steps go beyond every text cited. The first is reading a pay-as-you-go entitlement through ¶ I.3.9, so that its value depends on the goods future workers produce, the probability that they will be made to hand them over, and the wait. The second is likening unfunded promises to fiduciary media, as claims issued beyond any fund set aside to meet them. The third is putting cohort size into the Peter-and-Paul passage, which contains no population variable: the burden on Peter does not depend, in Mises's telling, on how many Peters there are.

**Strongest counter-evidence.** Aaron's condition is the decisive objection to any suggestion that a claim without a fund is hollow: an unfunded scheme is backed by the growth of the wage bill and beats a funded one whenever population growth plus wage growth exceeds the interest rate. The calibrated models reviewed in the next section have ageing lowering the interest rate as well as population growth, so the comparison does not move one-for-one against pay-as-you-go as a population ages (an inference from those models, not a finding of any one of them). Mises's own charge that social-security saving forms no capital is conditional on a premise he states, "If the government spends the hundred dollars for current expenditure" (¶ 6.XXXV.53). The fiduciary-media analogy fails on Mises's own criterion: a contributor does give up present goods, the entitlement is a statutory tax-transfer promise and not a demand claim, it does not circulate, and it creates no purchasing power, so the crisis mechanism has nothing to act on. Modern models also place unfunded promises inside the capital market: public debt and social insurance raise the neutral rate, by about two percentage points for debt in Eggertsson, Mehrotra and Robbins's decomposition as reported in a secondary summary ([Equitable Growth](https://equitablegrowth.org/whats-behind-the-decline-in-u-s-interest-rates/); [Rachel and Summers 2019](https://www.brookings.edu/bpea-articles/on-falling-neutral-real-rates-fiscal-policy-and-the-risk-of-secular-stagnation/)). Mises's own demographic worry ran the other way from the essay's. His remedy for low wages was "to increase the per-head quota of capital invested" (Part Four, ch. 21 §3, ¶ IV.21.35), and in the 1950 article "to accelerate the increase of capital as compared with population" ([*Planning for Freedom*, pp. 89–90](https://cdn.mises.org/Planning%20for%20Freedom%20and%20Twelve%20other%20Essays%20and%20Addresses.pdf)): he feared too many heads per unit of capital, not too few workers per retiree.

## An ageing society's low interest is a lower natural rate, not the gap behind Mises's crisis

**What Mises wrote.** Chapter 19 takes over Wicksell's two rates: the natural rate is "the rate of interest that would be determined by supply and demand if actual capital goods were lent without the mediation of money", and the money rate "the rate of interest that is demanded and paid for loans in money or money substitutes" (Part Three, ch. 19 §3, ¶ III.19.28; 1912) ([Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)). He gives the natural rate a real anchor in saving.

> "In fact, one of the elements that help to determine the rate of interest, the level of the national subsistence fund, is necessarily altered by the increase of savings. The greater the fund of means of subsistence in a community, the lower the rate of interest."
>
> Part Three, ch. 19 "Money, Credit, and Interest", §2, ¶ III.19.16; 1912 ([Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23))

The crisis theory concerns a different event. "Now if the rate of interest on loans is artificially reduced below the natural rate as established by the free play of the forces operating in the market, then entrepreneurs are enabled and obliged to enter upon longer processes of production" (ch. 19 §4, ¶ III.19.37), although the saving that would carry those processes has not been done. Mises draws the line himself: "We are not concerned with a reduction in the natural rate of interest brought about by an increase in the issue of fiduciary media, but with a reduction below this rate in the money rate charged by the banks" (ch. 19 §4, ¶ III.19.38). He also sets the benchmark in a world market. With mobile capital "the net rate of interest is no longer determined according to national, but according to international, considerations. Its level is settled, not by the natural rate of interest in the country, but by the natural rate of interest anywhere" (ch. 20 §3, ¶ III.20.13; 1912). France, "of all the countries in the whole world that which is richest in capital", shows the pattern: "Now in a country which exports capital, even disregarding the premium for risk-bearing that is contained in the gross rate of interest, the rate of interest on loans must be lower than in a country which imports capital", and of low French rates he says "The cause is a general economic cause; it is a matter in which measures of banking or currency policy can have no influence" (ch. 20 §4, ¶ III.20.20) ([Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)).

**What later writers added.** Everything demographic. Calibrated life-cycle models agree on the sign: demographic change lowered the equilibrium real rate by at least 1.5 percentage points between 1990 and 2014 ([Carvalho, Ferrero and Nechio](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/wp2016-05.pdf)), by about 1¼ points since 1980 ([Gagnon, Johannsen and López-Salido](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/understanding-the-new-normal-the-role-of-demographics.htm)) and by roughly 160 basis points across advanced economies between 1980 and 2015 ([Lisack, Sajedi and Thwaites](https://www.ijcb.org/journal/ijcb21q2a2.htm)), and the paper the essay already uses projects a further fall of **123 basis points in global returns by 2100** ([Auclert, Malmberg, Martenet and Rognlie](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29161)). The dissent is Goodhart and Pradhan: "As the world ages, real interest rates will rise, inflation and wage growth will pick up and inequality will fall", a forecast offered without a calibrated magnitude and on a stated political premise, "that the safety net will remain in place, keeping savings from rising proportionally with longevity" ([BIS Working Paper 656, pp. 1, 9](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-656-demographics-will-reverse-three-multi-decade-global-trends.pdf)). The modern estimated neutral rate is also a different object from the in-kind rate of ¶ III.19.28, and different again from the price-stabilising "normal rate" of Wicksell that Mises treats separately (ch. 19 §3, ¶ III.19.29); an essay sentence should speak of the rate that real saving and real capital would set, not of the rate consistent with stable prices.

**What would be the essay's own.** The bridge is conditional and it is worth building. If ageing raises desired saving relative to investment, then in the 1912 framework the natural rate itself falls (¶ III.19.16), and a low rate with that cause is, in Mises's own terms, not "artificially reduced". Only a money rate held below that lower natural rate would start the mechanism of ¶ III.19.37. **Read this way, the 1912 text gives no licence for treating every low interest rate as a central-bank distortion.** The second extension uses ¶ III.20.20 as a 1912 statement of the pattern in the essay's current-account material: capital-rich economies export capital and carry low rates for reasons monetary policy cannot change. Mises's causes are capital abundance, a lack of "the spirit of initiative and enterprise" and investors' home bias; he does not mention French fertility, and reading demography into the passage is the essayist's act.

**Strongest counter-evidence.** The best witness against this connection is Mises. The footnote attached to the chapter's first paragraph warns: "The fact that I have followed the terminology and method of attack of Böhm-Bawerk's theory of interest throughout this chapter does not imply that I am an adherent of that theory or am able to regard it as a satisfactory solution of the problem" (footnote to ¶ III.19.1, [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)). *Human Action* then reverses ¶ III.19.16 outright: "The quantity of the available supply of capital goods influences neither the rate of originary interest nor the amount of further saving" (ch. XIX §3, ¶ 4.XIX.29) ([Econlib, *Human Action* ch. XIX](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/HmA/msHmA.html?chapter_num=22)). Hülsmann's summary of the mature position is that "There is no such thing as a natural rate of interest, defined as the rate of interest that would prevail in a barter economy" ([Hülsmann 2007, pp. 779–780](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf)). The later Mises is a hostile witness for "more saving, lower natural rate", so the essay can cite only the 1912 chapter, by date.

The crisis theory that gives the distinction its point is also a minority view. Milton Friedman found no correlation between the size of an expansion and of the contraction after it, a result that "would cast grave doubt on those theories that see as the source of a deep depression the excesses of the prior expansion (the Mises cycle theory is a clear example)" (quoted in [Huerta de Soto, p. 495 n. 103](https://cdn.mises.org/Money_Bank_Credit_and_Economic_Cycles_De%20Soto.pdf)). Gordon Tullock's objection is that "the mechanism described by the Austrians would lead, not to unemployment, but to a shortage of labor and higher wages" ([Tullock 1989, pp. 147–149](https://cdn.mises.org/rae3_1_12.pdf)), and the one econometric test of the theory's distinctive stage-of-production predictions reported little evidence for them ([Lester and Wolff 2013](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:revaec:v:26:y:2013:i:4:p:433-461)). What mainstream evidence supports is a weaker cousin: "credit booms tend to undermine productivity growth by inducing labour reallocations towards lower productivity growth sectors" ([BIS Working Paper 534](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-534-labour-reallocation-and-productivity-dynamics-financial-causes-real-consequences.pdf)). Even Hayek, who built on the chapter, wrote that Mises's statement of the mechanism came to "hardly more than the sentences quoted at the beginning of this lecture" and that they "remained completely unintelligible to all who were not very familiar with Böhm-Bawerk's theory of interest" ([Hayek 1935, pp. 69–70 n. 1](https://cdn.mises.org/Prices%20and%20Production_2.pdf)). Keynes reviewed the German original in 1914 as "critical rather than constructive, dialectical and not original" (as transcribed by [Machlup](https://www.econlib.org/library/NPDBooks/Moss/mslLvM.html?chapter_num=3)).

## Inflation settles the borrowing, not the indexed pension

**What Mises wrote.** Four separate claims are in play, and they carry different dates. The first is the contract: "An increase in the purchasing power of money is disadvantageous to the debtor and advantageous to the creditor; a decrease in its purchasing power has the contrary significance" (Part Two, ch. 12 §1, ¶ II.12.11; 1912), with its legal corollary: "When notes that are appraised commercially at only half their face value are proclaimed legal tender, this amounts fundamentally to the same thing as granting debtors legal relief from half of their liabilities" (Part One, ch. 4 §2, ¶ I.4.9) ([Econlib ch. 12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16); [ch. 4](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=8)). The second is the motive, written in 1924 about 1914–23.

> "A government always finds itself obliged to resort to inflationary measures when it cannot negotiate loans and dare not levy taxes, because it has reason to fear that it will forfeit approval of the policy it is following if it reveals too soon the financial and general economic consequences of that policy."
>
> Part Two, ch. 13 "Monetary Policy", §3 "Inflationism", ¶ II.13.18; 1924 ([Econlib ch. 13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17))

The same paragraph calls inflation "an instrument of unpopular, that is, of antidemocratic, policy" and ends "That is the political function of inflation". The expenditures Mises names around it are war, cheap bread, loss-making state railways, the unemployed and reparations; pensions are not among them. War is his leading case: "Who has any doubt that the belligerent peoples of Europe would have tired of war much more quickly if their governments had clearly and candidly laid before them at the time the account of their war expenditure?" (ch. 13 §3, ¶ II.13.16; 1924). The third claim is the constitutional version of 1952: sound money "Ideologically ... belongs in the same class with political constitutions and bills of rights" (Part Four, ch. 21 §1, ¶ IV.21.4), because "Parliamentary control of finances works only if the government is not in a position to provide for unauthorized expenditures by increasing the circulating amount of fiat money" (ch. 21 §2, ¶ IV.21.13); inflation supplies "the funds which it could neither collect by taxation nor borrow from the savings of the public because the people and its parliamentary representatives objected" (ch. 21 §4, ¶ IV.21.48), in a polity whose citizens "vote, for instance, only for such taxes as are to be paid by other people, namely, the rich" (ch. 21 §4, ¶ IV.21.46) ([Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25)). The fourth claim says who pays.

> "The typical creditors are not the rich but people of modest means who own bonds and savings accounts or have taken out insurance policies. If the common man supports anticreditor measures, he does it because he ignores the fact that he himself is a creditor."
>
> Part Four, ch. 21 "The Principle of Sound Money", §2 "The Virtues and Alleged Shortcomings of the Gold Standard", ¶ IV.21.17; 1952 ([Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25))

Mises limits each claim himself, and the limits are the part of the text most useful to an honest essay. "Depreciation of money can benefit debtors only when it is unforeseen" (ch. 13 §3, ¶ II.13.11; 1924), because foreseen changes "could be sufficiently allowed for in the original terms of the credit transaction" (ch. 12 §1, ¶ II.12.14; 1924). The treasury was not, in his view, the chief motive of his own day: "The main inflationary motive of our day is the so-called full-employment policy, not the treasury's incapacity to fill its empty vaults from sources other than bank loans" (Part Four, ch. 23 §2, ¶ IV.23.20), and of the devaluations of 1931–34 he had written that "the motive for recent experiments in depreciation has been by no means fiscal" (Preface to the English Edition, ¶ HP.6; 1934) ([Econlib ch. 23](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=27); [Econlib prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4)). Sound money in his sense sets no ceiling on promises: "Whether governments should borrow from the public at all and, if so, to what extent are questions that are irrelevant to the treatment of monetary problems" (ch. 23 §2, ¶ IV.23.12). He ranks the creditor's loss below the harm done by uneven price changes (ch. 23 §5, ¶¶ IV.23.67–68). And against his own warnings that "Consistently and uninterruptedly continued inflation must eventually lead to collapse" (Part Two, ch. 13 §3, ¶ II.13.25; 1924) and that all countries faced "the gloomy prospect of a complete breakdown of their currencies" (Preface to the New Edition, ¶ P.1; 1952), he concedes that a managed paper currency "can last and is not inevitably headed for a breakdown" (Part Four, ch. 22 §3, ¶ IV.22.5) ([Econlib ch. 22](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=26)).

**What later writers added.** Magnitudes, mechanisms and the age of the creditor. Reinhart and Sbrancia measured the post-war "liquidation effect" at **2.3% of GDP a year in the United States and 3.0% in the United Kingdom over 1945–1980**, and found that "Inflation need not take market participants entirely by surprise and, in effect, it need not be very high (by historic standards)"; only a quarter of their liquidation years coincided with an inflation surprise ([BIS Working Paper 363, abstract, pp. 26, 28](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-363-liquidation-government-debt.pdf)). On a fair reading that qualifies ¶ II.13.11 without refuting it: his rule assumes lenders free to reprice, and interest ceilings with capital controls removed the freedom. Acalin and Ball estimate that United States debt, which fell from 106% of GDP in 1946 to 23% in 1974, would have fallen only to about 73–74% without primary surpluses and interest-rate distortions ([NBER Working Paper 31577](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31577)). The record of 1914–18 bears out Mises's own case: Britain and the United States each covered about a quarter of war costs by taxation, "while in Austria the contribution of taxation towards the expenditure was zero" ([Wikipedia, War finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_finance)). The forward-looking version is the BIS's: "age-related expenditures will grow by approximately 4% and 5% of GDP, respectively, over the next 20 years. Absent fiscal consolidation, this would push debt above 200% and 150% of GDP by 2050" in advanced and emerging economies, with fiscal dominance the named risk ([BIS Annual Economic Report 2023, ch. II, pp. 56, 61–62](https://www.bis.org/publications/monetary-fiscal-policy-safeguarding-stability-trust.pdf)). Goodhart and Pradhan make ageing itself inflationary: "Inflation will be the inevitable consequence of the tax wedge that plugs the gap between inadequate savings and rising spending" ([BIS Working Paper 656, p. 18](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-656-demographics-will-reverse-three-multi-decade-global-trends.pdf)). Doepke and Schneider supply the demography of ¶ IV.21.17: the main losers from unexpected inflation in the United States are rich, old households, the major bondholders, and the main winners are young, middle-class households with fixed-rate mortgages, with the government gaining and foreigners losing ([Doepke and Schneider 2006](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:v:114:y:2006:i:6:p:1069-1097)). That supports Mises on age and cuts against "not the rich but people of modest means".

**What would be the essay's own.** The substitution of pension and health promises for war, bread and railways in ¶ II.13.18 is the essayist's; so is the phrase "a tax nobody votes for", a fair gloss of ¶¶ II.13.15–18 that is not Mises's wording (he reports "inflation is a kind of taxation" as an argument made by inflation's defenders, ch. 13 §3, ¶ II.13.14). So is the two-step version that survives the evidence: unfunded age-related spending raises deficits, deficits become nominal debt, and nominal debt can be liquidated by repression with chronic inflation. On that reading inflation settles the borrowing done to keep the promises, and the loss falls on holders of nominal claims, who are disproportionately the old and their pension funds. **"Inflating away promises to the old" then means taxing the old's savings to keep paying the old's pensions.** For readers of geopolitics the essay can add a further step of its own. Mark Haas argued in 2007 that ageing would crowd military spending out of every great power's budget ([Haas 2007](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/geriatric-peace-future-us-power-world-aging-populations)), yet world military expenditure reached a record $2,887 billion in 2025, with Germany's up 24% and Japan's up 9.7% ([SIPRI](https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2026/global-military-spending-surges)). Rearmament laid on top of fixed pension promises is financed by borrowing, which recreates the situation of ¶ II.13.18; the security literature does not draw that link, and neither does Mises.

**Strongest counter-evidence.** Japan and indexation. The oldest large economy, whose public debt of about 240% of GDP in 2013 was the highest of any nation ([Wikipedia, Lost Decades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades)), saw consumer prices fall **4.1% between fiscal 1998 and fiscal 2012** ([Bank of Japan, Kuroda speech, 2013](https://www.boj.or.jp/en/about/press/koen_2013/data/ko131010a.pdf)). It cut pensions by an explicit legislated formula, the 2004 "macroeconomic slide", which is not applied when it would reduce benefits in nominal terms and so could not operate until April 2015 ([JCER](https://www.jcer.or.jp/english/the-japanese-pension-system-its-long-term-prospects)). More than half of OECD countries index pensions in payment so that they are fully protected against inflation over time ([OECD, *Pensions at a Glance 2023*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2023_678055dd-en/full-report/component-5.html)). The recorded settlement of pension promises is by rule: the average normal retirement age in the OECD rises from 64.7 and 63.9 for men and women retiring in 2024 to 66.4 and 65.9 for those starting work then ([OECD, *Pensions at a Glance 2025*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2025_e40274c1-en/full-report/executive-summary_68260caa.html)), about two-thirds of OECD countries run some automatic adjustment mechanism ([OECD, *Pensions at a Glance 2021*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2021_ca401ebd-en.html)), and Greece, inside the euro with no monetary exit, cut nominal pension spending by 9.1% over 2010–2014 ([LSE Hellenic Observatory](https://www.lse.ac.uk/Hellenic-Observatory/Assets/Documents/Publications/Books/2015-17/greece-expenditure-on-social-protection-and-pensions.pdf)). The arithmetic is also weak: inflation alone would do little to the United States debt burden because the debt is short-dated ([Hilscher, Raviv and Reis 2022](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:35:y:2022:i:3:p:1553-1595.)), and the IMF finds that a one-point inflation surprise lowers public debt by 0.6 points of GDP where debt exceeds 50% of GDP while expected inflation lowers it not at all ([IMF Fiscal Monitor, April 2023](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/FM/Issues/2023/04/03/fiscal-monitor-april-2023)). The politics point the same way, since inflation's losers are the growing voting bloc: Bullard, Garriga and Waller model older cohorts as preferring low inflation ([St. Louis Fed Review 2012](https://fedinprint.org/item/fedlrv/24667)) and Vlandas finds older countries have lower inflation ([Vlandas 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0010414017710261)), although the longest panel study rejects the outcome claim, finding that "the elderly, except for the very old, are inflationary not disinflationary" ([BIS Working Paper 722, p. 19](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-722-enduring-link-between-demography-and-inflation.pdf)).

Two further points complete the ledger. Mises's 1950 sentence that "In an industrial society all deferred payments must be stipulated in terms of money" ([*Planning for Freedom*, p. 89](https://cdn.mises.org/Planning%20for%20Freedom%20and%20Twelve%20other%20Essays%20and%20Addresses.pdf)) predates statutory indexation; United States Social Security acquired its automatic cost-of-living adjustment by a 1972 law that took effect in 1975 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Social_Security_in_the_United_States)), and the book had rejected indexation as a cure from its first edition (ch. 12 §1, ¶¶ II.12.15–16). The evidence on the other side is recent and Japanese: gross debt fell from 229% of GDP at the pandemic peak to an estimated 207% by the third quarter of 2025 as nominal growth outran the effective interest rate ([IMF, April 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/04/02/pr-26105-japan-imf-executive-board-concludes-2026-article-iv-consult)), while pensions rose 1.9% in fiscal 2025, less than prices for a third year ([Nippon.com](https://nippon.com/en/news/yjj2025012400541)). Inflation there is the condition under which a legislated rule finally bites, and it arrived with the global surge of 2021–23 and a weak yen; no source shows that it was chosen.

## Reserves as claims on one government: a 1924 structure with a two-way modern record

**What Mises wrote.** The 1924 chapter on post-war credit policy describes a reserve pyramid in terms that need little translation.

> "The next step was the adoption of the practice by a series of states of holding the gold reserves of the central banks-of-issue (or the redemption funds that took their place), not in actual gold, but in various sorts of foreign claims to gold."
>
> "Fluctuations in the price of gold are nowadays substantially dependent on the behavior of one government, namely, that of the United States."
>
> Part Three, ch. 20 "Problems of Credit Policy", §9 "The Gold-Exchange Standard", ¶ III.20.42; 1924 ([Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24))

The same paragraph adds "But this policy of the United States, which involves considerable sacrifices, might one day be changed", and the next says such a standard "must at last mean depriving gold of that characteristic which is the most important from the point of view of monetary policy--its independence of government influence upon fluctuations in its value" (ch. 20 §9, ¶ III.20.43). He notes that his own country was already there: "Thus Austria has a dollar-exchange standard and so, indirectly, a gold-exchange standard" (ch. 20 §9, ¶ III.20.41). The system cannot be generalised: "It is clear that the policy of investing the whole redemption fund in foreign claims to gold cannot become universal" (ch. 18 §8, ¶ III.18.42; 1924) ([Econlib ch. 18](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=22)). Three other passages bear on nations as creditors. New money is paid for by whoever receives it last: "Those countries that are the last to be reached by the new stream of money are those which must ultimately bear the cost of the increased welfare of the other countries" (Part Two, ch. 12 §3, ¶ II.12.35). A currency is not a share in national wealth: "even the richest country may have a bad currency and the poorest country a good one" (ch. 14 §2, ¶ II.14.9; 1924). And a country that imports more than it exports in goods pays in claims: "then they must export shares, bonds, and securities of various kinds" (ch. 14 §4, ¶ II.14.24) ([Econlib ch. 12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16); [ch. 14](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=18)).

Part Four, written in 1952 with the International Monetary Fund already at work, is thinner on the international order than its title suggests. "The outstanding instance of a freely vacillating currency today is the United States dollar, the New Deal dollar" (ch. 22 §3, ¶ IV.22.8), yet a poor country may peg to it: once "Ruritania is either on the gold-exchange standard or on the dollar-exchange standard", Mises judges, "This is enough for the beginning" (ch. 23 §3, ¶ IV.23.34). His 1952 objection to the gold-exchange standard is domestic: "It offers to governments an easy opportunity to embark upon inflation unbeknown to the nation" (ch. 23 §4, ¶ IV.23.58). The Fund earns one clause, "the manifest futility of the International Monetary Fund" (ch. 23 §1, ¶ IV.23.2), and a search of Part Four and the prefaces finds no "reserve currency", "Bretton", "Marshall" or "foreign aid" ([Econlib ch. 22](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=26); [ch. 23](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=27)).

**What later writers added.** The whole analysis of a reserve currency. The Triffin dilemma dates from the 1960s, as does Valéry Giscard d'Estaing's phrase "exorbitant privilege" ([Wikipedia, Triffin dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triffin_dilemma); [Wikipedia, Exorbitant privilege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorbitant_privilege)). Gourinchas and Rey documented the issuer's excess return on its foreign assets over its liabilities ([NBER Working Paper 11563](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11563)), and BIS authors state the creditor's side: a dollar decline works "by redistributing wealth from the rest of the world to US residents" ([BIS Working Paper 684, p. 20](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-684-triffin-dilemma-or-myth.pdf)). The positions are large, and the three biggest creditors are ageing economies. At the end of 2025 Germany held net external assets of **EUR 3,661 billion, about 81% of GDP** ([Bundesbank](https://www.bundesbank.de/en/press/press-releases/germany-s-international-investment-position-at-the-end-of-2025-1009828)), China $4,071 billion ([SAFE](https://www.safe.gov.cn/en/2026/0327/2405.html)) and Japan JPY 561.75 trillion ([Ministry of Finance](https://www.mof.go.jp/english/policy/international_policy/reference/iip/e2025.htm)), against a United States net liability of about $27.5 trillion ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/us-international-transactions-and-investment-position-4th-quarter-and-year-2025)). The dollar's share of allocated reserves stood near **57% in 2025–26 against about 71% in 1999–2001** ([IMF COFER as transcribed by Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Template:Reserve_currencies); [Central Banking](https://www.centralbanking.com/central-banks/economics/data/7977210/dollars-share-of-foreign-reserves-fell-slightly-in-q2-imf)), while "At end-2025 gold accounted for more than 20% of global official reserves" ([BIS Working Paper 1380, p. 2](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-1380-chasing-el-dorado-gold-under-shifting-geopolitical-and-financial-condition.pdf)). Non-US investors held 33% of United States Treasury securities in March 2025, and "the hedge ratio for major Japanese life insurers declined from about 60% in 2021 to 40% in 2024" ([BIS Bulletin 105, pp. 1, 3](https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-105-us-dollars-slide-april-2025-role-fx-hedging.pdf)), which ties the exposure to institutions that fund retirement promises in yen. The historical claim nearest to Mises's theme is Chen and co-authors', as summarised by Wikipedia: "When exorbitant privilege is lost, investors into government bonds suffer substantial losses and the affected government resorts to financial repression" ([Wikipedia, Exorbitant privilege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorbitant_privilege)). One study bears directly on ageing: Germany's capital exports do little to diversify demographic risk because they go mainly to countries with similar demographics ([Hünnekes, Schularick and Trebesch](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/13863.html)).

**What would be the essay's own.** Two links. The first joins age to creditor status: Mises explains a capital exporter by capital abundance, and nothing in the book ties surplus saving to an age structure. The second reads an old creditor nation's net foreign assets as its retirement saving; no source was found that measures the share of pension or household retirement assets held in foreign-currency claims, so the step from a national balance sheet to "pensions" is an inference from who the large foreign investors are. The analogy that the essay can defend is one of structure: in 1924 the claim was a claim to gold whose value hung on Federal Reserve policy, and today the claim is a fiat dollar.

**Strongest counter-evidence.** The text first. Mises's fear in ¶ III.20.42 was that the United States would stop absorbing gold and let its value fall, a different mechanism from an issuer depreciating its own paper; his 1952 objection is about what citizens can see, not about an issuer financing deficits out of other countries' reserves; and in 1934 he rejected the very framing: "It consequently seems misleading in discussions of the currency question to talk of an opposition between the interests of creditor and debtor nations, of those which are well supplied with capital and those which are ill supplied" (Preface to the English Edition, ¶ HP.12), on the ground that poorer countries depend on imported capital and lose most when international credit is throttled ([Econlib prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4)). The record second: transfers have run in both directions. Creditors lost when Bretton Woods failed "once the US began following inflationary policies in the mid-1960s" ([BIS Working Paper 684, p. 7](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-684-triffin-dilemma-or-myth.pdf)) and again in April 2025, when "investors faced losses on the unhedged portion of their dollar portfolio" ([BIS Bulletin 105, p. 4](https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-105-us-dollars-slide-april-2025-role-fx-hedging.pdf)). They gained in 2007–09, when the United States transferred about 20% of its GDP to the rest of the world ([Gourinchas, Rey and Govillot](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/16944.html)), in 2021–24, when "The trend appreciation of the US dollar between 2021 and 2024 encouraged non-US investors to leave their dollar portfolios unhedged to benefit from currency gains" ([BIS Bulletin 105, p. 3](https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-105-us-dollars-slide-april-2025-role-fx-hedging.pdf)), and through the United States equity boom, which is the main reason the American net liability reached about 60% of GDP by 2023 ([Atkeson, Heathcote and Perri 2025](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:115:y:2025:i:7:p:2151-2206)). Foreigners now hold more United States equity than bonds, $17.6 trillion against $13.6 trillion in March 2025 ([BIS Bulletin 105, p. 1](https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-105-us-dollars-slide-april-2025-role-fx-hedging.pdf)), so the inflate-away story applies to the bond portion only. The reserve data show no flight: the dollar share moved from 59.40% at the end of 2021 to 58.52% at the end of 2024, under one point in the three years after about $300 billion of Russian reserves were immobilised ([Wikipedia, Template: Reserve currencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Template:Reserve_currencies); [Wikipedia, International sanctions during the Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_sanctions_during_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine)). And the current-account version of Triffin's dilemma is, in Bordo and McCauley's words, "popular, but anachronistic, and flawed in logic and fact" ([BIS Working Paper 684, p. 1](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-684-triffin-dilemma-or-myth.pdf)).

The ranking weighs three things: how directly a sentence of the book states the point, how much of the modern evidence the point organises, and how cheaply its main caveat can be stated inside 250 to 400 words.

Part One, ch. 3 "The Various Kinds of Money", §1 "Money and Money Substitutes", ¶ I.3.9 | The book never applies this to pensions; the sentence that does is in *Human Action* (1949), and Aaron's condition shows an unfunded claim is backed by the growth of the wage bill |

The first connection carries a section alone. Its spine is three moves: the 1924 sentence with its date, the correction from indexation and Japan, and the restated claim that inflation in an old society taxes the old's savings to keep paying the old's pensions, with ¶ IV.21.17 available as a second quotation if space allows. Two paragraphs near the constitutional passage (¶¶ IV.21.3 and IV.21.48) carry 1952 civilisational language the essay will not want, so ¶ IV.21.4 should be quoted alone if it is quoted. The second connection suits the essay's existing treatment of Auclert and co-authors, where its job is negative: it stops a reader from filing a demographic fall in returns under central-bank distortion, and it must cite the 1912 chapter by date because the 1949 Mises contradicts it. The third belongs beside the essay's account of the post-war monetary order and needs a period attached to every statement that creditors lose. The fourth stands only as a partner to the first: its definition makes a good opening line, and the pension sentence must be attributed to *Human Action* and not to this book.

Three candidates fall below the line. The cycle theory itself explains nothing about ageing, since a pension promise creates no bank credit and the theory is rejected by most economists. The gold standard as a remedy fails on the evidence: the restraint that stops a government inflating also stopped monetary authorities offsetting banking panics between the wars, leaving gold helped countries recover from the Depression, and 92% of economists in a 2012 survey agreed that a return to gold would not improve price stability and employment ([Wikipedia, Gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard)). The resemblance between late receivers of new money and later-born cohorts is one of shape only: Mises's sequence is the order in which money is received, the essay's is birth order, and the mechanisms differ.

## Conclusion

Mises supplies a grammar and no quantities. His sentences ask of any promise who issued it, against what fund, who can be made to accept less, and whether the electorate can see the bill; demography then says how large the promise is and how many will pay. The grammar's most valuable output for the essay is negative. It forbids two tempting sentences: that low interest in an old society is a central-bank artefact, and that old societies will inflate away their pensions. The three vintages of the text also map onto three voices, the pre-war theorist of real resources and the natural rate, the 1924 witness to hyperinflation who explains why governments inflate, and the 1952 polemicist who identifies the creditor with the common saver, and a section that blends them should date each sentence.

The open question is one Mises's own text poses against itself. His 1924 motive predicts that states facing projected age-related debt above 200% of GDP will reach for the printing press; his 1952 observation implies that the electorate deciding the matter is, increasingly, an electorate of creditors. Japan between 1998 and 2012 is consistent with the second force prevailing, and Japan since 2022 is a mild version of the first, arriving from abroad. The essay can state that contest precisely and should not pretend to settle it. Before publication, every figure here that came from a search extract (the OECD, IMF, NBER, national-accounts and central-bank numbers, and the journal abstracts) needs checking on its own page, and so does the essay's journal citation for Auclert and co-authors, which this research could confirm only as an NBER working paper.

## Addendum: what two rounds of review changed (19 November 2026)

The essay section built on this report was reviewed twice, each finding checked by a second reader against the sources. Five points correct or extend the report itself.

- **The chapter 19 footnote is 1924 text.** The working notes could not date it. Hülsmann (2012, p. 23 n. 46) lists it among the changes in the second edition, and it is absent from the 1912 German first edition (p. 401), which was opened to check.
- ***Human Action* does not simply reverse the saving sentence.** Chapter XIX §3 says the quantity of capital goods "influences neither the rate of originary interest nor the amount of further saving", but chapter XX §5 allows that forced saving "lowers the rate of originary interest". The accurate summary is that the 1949 book denies that more capital means lower interest and keeps the distinction between the two rates under other names.
- **Japan's debt ratio is the highest among large economies, not of all countries.** IMF figures for 2025 (World Economic Outlook, April 2026, read through Wikipedia's table) put Venezuela higher.
- **Japan since 2023 belongs in the evidence.** Its legislated rule raised pensions by less than prices in fiscal 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026 (search-extract grade; ministry pages could not be opened). This is the strongest fact against the claim that old societies will not let inflation touch pensions.
- **Mises's condition in ¶ II.13.18 is a government that cannot borrow.** Japan could, so its falling prices are evidence against the essay's own bridge from ageing to inflation, not against that sentence.
