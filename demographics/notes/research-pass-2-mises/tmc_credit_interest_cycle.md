## 1. Mises's own definitions: money in the narrower sense, money substitutes, money certificates, fiduciary media, commodity credit, circulation credit

### Takeaway
Part III defines the two kinds of credit and restates fiduciary media as "notes and bank balances that are not covered by money" [III.15.1]; the formal definitions of money substitutes, money certificates and money in the narrower/broader sense sit earlier, in Part I ch. 3 and Part II ch. 8, and Part III only uses them. Commodity credit (Sachkredit) costs the lender present goods; circulation credit (Zirkulationskredit) is granted by issuing demand claims and costs the issuer nothing.

#### Money certificates and fiduciary media
- Formal definition: "We shall use the term money certificates for those money substitutes that are completely covered by the reservation of corresponding sums of money, and the term fiduciary media for those which are not covered in this way." - Partly covered issues are split pro rata: "each individual note is to be reckoned as two-thirds fiduciary medium and one-third money certificate" [II.8.57], Part II ch. 8 §7. — [Econlib Part II ch. 8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
- Part III, first sentence: "The business of banking falls into two distinct branches: the negotiation of credit through the loan of other people's money and the granting of credit through the issue of fiduciary media, that is, notes and bank balances that are not covered by money." [1912] — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- Effect on the money stock: "Fiduciary media increase the supply of money in the broader sense of the word; they are consequently able to influence the objective exchange value of money." #### Commodity credit and circulation credit (all ch. 15 §3 unless stated; [1912])
- Credit as such: "A credit transaction is an exchange of present goods for future goods." [III.15.9]. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- First group (lender sacrifices): transactions in which "they impose a sacrifice on that party who performs his part of the bargain before the other does" [III.15.10]; "The exchange ratio embodied in the contract contains an expression of the value of time in the opinions of the individuals concerned." [III.15.10]. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- Second group (no sacrifice): "The second group of credit transactions is characterized by the fact that in them the gain of the party who receives before he pays is balanced by no sacrifice on the part of the other party." [III.15.11]; "If a creditor is able to confer a loan by issuing claims which are payable on demand, then the granting of the credit is bound up with no economic sacrifice for him." [III.15.11]. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- The names: "For the first group the name commodity credit (Sachkredit) is suggested, for the second the name circulation credit (Zirkulationskredit)." [III.15.12]. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- The decisive test, a fund that did not exist before: "The only circumstance that is of importance here is that the loans are granted out of a fund that did not exist before the loans were granted." and "In all other circumstances, whenever loans are granted they are granted out of existing and available funds of wealth." [III.15.23], ch. 15 §4. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- Genuine credit is bounded by present goods: "the total of the possible loans is limited by the total quantity of money and other goods available for this purpose" and "Loans can be granted only by those who have disposal over money or other economic goods which they can do without for a period." [III.15.27], ch. 15 §5. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- Neither depositor nor noteholder is a lender: "A depositor of a sum of money who acquires in exchange for it a claim convertible into money at any time which will perform exactly the same service for him as the sum it refers to, has exchanged no present good for a future good." [III.15.18], ch. 15 §4; "A person who accepts and holds notes, grants no credit; he exchanges no present good for a future good." [III.15.24], ch. 15 §5. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- Who gains: "The other part arises from the granting of circulation credit. It is the bank that makes this profit, not the holders of the notes." [III.15.25], ch. 15 §5. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- First effect on interest: "The bank-of-issue does not demand credit; it grants it." and the new notes are "an increase in the supply of credit and has therefore an immediate tendency to diminish the rate of interest" (exact words: "it constitutes an increase in the supply of credit and has therefore an immediate tendency to diminish the rate of interest") [III.15.29], ch. 15 §5. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)
- Savings deposits are the other branch (intermediation, no money creation): "The sums of money handed over to the bank by its customers in this branch of business are not a part of their reserves, but investments of money which are not necessary for day-to-day transactions." [III.15.21], ch. 15 §4; "Only those who lend the money of others are bankers; those who merely lend their own capital are capitalists, but not bankers." [III.15.5], ch. 15 §2. — [Econlib ch. 15](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=19)

### Inferences
- The exact contrast the essay wants ("a lender gives up present goods" vs "created with no one's sacrifice") is Mises's own, in III.15.10-11; the safest single quotation is III.15.11's first sentence, and the safest one-line gloss of fiduciary media is the III.15.1 clause "notes and bank balances that are not covered by money".
- "With no one's sacrifice" needs one qualifier to be faithful: no sacrifice by the issuer at the moment of lending. Mises says elsewhere that the cost falls on third parties through the fall in money's purchasing power (III.17.30, quoted under Key Question 3).
- The Appendix B tree (money certificates under "money substitutes", hence inside "money in the broader sense") and II.8.57 (money certificates counted with money in the narrower sense when adding up demand, to avoid counting the cover twice) are two different cuts. If the essay defines "money in the narrower sense", use Mises's own II.8.57 wording or simply "money proper" rather than the diagram.
- "Commodity credit" is Batson's rendering of Sachkredit; it does not mean credit granted in commodities. A reader may misread it that way, so give the German or the definition.

## 2. Chapter 19: Wicksell's natural and money rates, what determines the natural rate, and why a bank-induced fall in the money rate cannot last

### Takeaway
Mises accepts Wicksell's distinction (natural rate = the rate if real capital were lent in kind; money rate = the rate on money loans) and Wicksell's claim that the two must converge, but rejects Wicksell's mechanism and supplies his own: a money rate below the natural rate makes longer production processes look profitable although the subsistence fund has not grown, consumption-goods prices then rise relative to production-goods prices, money depreciates, the loan rate is driven back up, and part of the lengthened investment is lost. The natural rate itself is fixed in Bohm-Bawerk's terms, by the subsistence fund and the marginal productivity of lengthening the period of production.

### Cited Findings

#### Statement of the two rates
- Wicksell's definitions as Mises reports them [III.19.28], ch. 19 §3 [1912]: the natural rate (natürliche Kapitalzins) is "the rate of interest that would be determined by supply and demand if actual capital goods were lent without the mediation of money", and the money rate (Geldzins) is "the rate of interest that is demanded and paid for loans in money or money substitutes" (both strings exact; each is preceded in the text by "or"). — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Earlier, in Mises's own voice, the natural rate is "that rate of interest that would be established by supply and demand if the real capital were lent in natura without the mediation of money" (within the phrase "below the natural rate of interest, that is below that rate of interest that would be established by supply and demand if the real capital were lent in natura without the mediation of money") [III.17.17], ch. 17 §4; footnote 38 there cites Wicksell, op. cit., p. v. — [Econlib ch. 17](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=21)
- Convergence accepted, mechanism disputed: "Nevertheless, it is certain that the money rate of interest must sooner or later come to the level of the natural rate of interest, and the problem is to say in what way this ultimate coincidence is brought about." and "Up to this point Wicksell commands assent; but his further argument provokes contradiction." [III.19.28], ch. 19 §3. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Mises separates Wicksell's price-stable "normal rate" from the natural rate (III.19.29) and objects to Wicksell's account of why banks would raise rates: "But it still remains inconceivable why a general rise in commodity prices should induce the banks to raise their rates of interest." [III.19.29], ch. 19 §3. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Other labels Mises uses for the same benchmark: "the rate determined at the time by the whole economic situation" (glossed in the text as Wicksell's natural rate) [III.19.35], ch. 19 §4; "the level determined by the circumstances of the capital market, that is, the market in which present goods and future goods are exchanged for one another" [III.19.21], ch. 19 §3; "equilibrium rate" appears in the §3 heading and once in ch. 20: "underbid the equilibrium rate of interest" [III.20.58], ch. 20 §12 [1924]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23); [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)
- Secondary check on Wicksell: Wikipedia gives two glosses on one page, "Wicksell invented the key term natural rate of interest and defined it as that interest rate which is compatible with a stable price level." and "The natural rate is the return on capital – or the real profit rate. It can be roughly considered to be equivalent to the marginal product of new capital. The money rate, in turn, is the loan rate, an entirely financial construction." Hulsmann notes the same doubleness: Wicksell defined the natural rate both as the rate under "real (non-monetary) factors" and as "the rate at which the price level would remain constant" (p. 253 n. 79). — [Wikipedia, Knut Wicksell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knut_Wicksell); [Hulsmann p. 253](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf)

#### What determines interest and the natural rate
- Origin of interest: "The margin by which the value of capital goods falls short of that of their expected products constitutes interest; its origin lies in the natural difference of value between present goods and future goods." [III.19.1], ch. 19 §1 [1912]. Footnote 56 (the Bohm-Bawerk disclaimer, see Key Question 7) hangs on this sentence. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Money scarcity is capital scarcity: "What is usually called plentifulness of money and scarcity of money is really plentifulness of capital and scarcity of capital." [III.19.2], ch. 19 §1. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- The subsistence fund: "In fact, one of the elements that help to determine the rate of interest, the level of the national subsistence fund, is necessarily altered by the increase of savings. The greater the fund of means of subsistence in a community, the lower the rate of interest." [III.19.16], ch. 19 §2 (footnote 67: Bohm-Bawerk, Kapital und Kapitalzins, p. 622); "This alteration in the size of the national subsistence fund is the most immediate cause of the variation which occurs in the rate of interest" [III.19.20], ch. 19 §3. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- The formal statement: "The level of the natural rate of interest is limited by the productivity of that lengthening of the period of production which is just justifiable economically and of that additional lengthening of the period of production which is just not justifiable" [III.19.37], ch. 19 §4; "The period of production which is thus defined must be of such a length that exactly the whole available subsistence fund is necessary on the one hand and sufficient on the other for paying the wages of the laborers throughout the duration of the productive process." [III.19.37] (footnotes 75-76: Bohm-Bawerk, pp. 611 ff. and 151 ff.). — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- No simple link from money to interest: "There is no direct connection between the rate of interest and the amount of money held by the individuals who participate in the transactions of the market" [III.19.12], ch. 19 §2; "it cannot be asserted that an increase in the stock of money causes the rate of interest to fall and a diminution of the stock of money causes it to rise" [III.19.16]; "the rate of interest could never be reduced to zero" [III.19.20], ch. 19 §3. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Transitional price-premium effect: "Thus a falling value of money goes hand in hand with a rising rate of interest, and a rising value of money with a falling rate of interest." [III.19.17], ch. 19 §2 (footnote 68: Fisher and Brown, The Purchasing Power of Money, 1911). — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- In an open economy the benchmark is international: capital mobility "has led to the formation of a homogeneous world capital market"; "the net rate of interest is no longer determined according to national, but according to international, considerations. Its level is settled, not by the natural rate of interest in the country, but by the natural rate of interest anywhere." [III.20.13], ch. 20 §3 [1912]. — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)

#### The banks' power, and the question
- The question: "Can the rate of interest be affected by the credit policy of the banks that issue fiduciary media?" and "The question that confronts us here is the much discussed question of the gratuitous nature of bank credit." [III.19.1]; "It is the chief problem in the theory of banking." [III.19.21], ch. 19 §3. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Against the Banking School: "the demand for credit is not a fixed quantity; it expands as the rate of interest falls, and contracts as the rate of interest rises" [III.19.9], ch. 19 §1; "by reducing the rate of interest charged on loans, it is possible for the banks indefinitely to increase the public demand for credit" and, for a single bank of issue, "In such a situation there are no other limits to the bank's issue of fiduciary media than those which it sets itself." [III.19.26], ch. 19 §3. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- First-round effect: new fiduciary media "are an additional supply of present goods and consequently they tend to cause the rate of interest to fall" (exact: "They are an additional supply of present goods and consequently they tend to cause the rate of interest to fall.") [III.19.22], ch. 19 §3; "Thus the rate of interest on loans may at first be reduced by the credit-issuing banks almost to zero." [III.19.32], ch. 19 §4. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Only the downward divergence matters: "We obviously need only consider the case in which the banks reduce the rate of interest below the natural rate." A rate above it means the banks "would simply withdraw from the competition of the loan market, without occasioning any other noteworthy consequences" [III.19.36], ch. 19 §4. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)

#### The cycle argument, sentence by sentence (ch. 19 §4, III.19.37-43; [1912])
- Step 1, longer processes are induced: "Now if the rate of interest on loans is artificially reduced below the natural rate as established by the free play of the forces operating in the market, then entrepreneurs are enabled and obliged to enter upon longer processes of production." [III.19.37]; "On the other hand, a reduction of the rate of interest on loans must necessarily lead to a lengthening of the average period of production." [III.19.37]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Step 2, the real precondition is missing: "A lengthening of the period of production is only practicable, however, either when the means of subsistence have increased sufficiently to support the laborers and entrepreneurs during the longer period or when the wants of producers have decreased sufficiently to enable them to make the same means of subsistence do for the longer period." [III.19.38]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Step 3, the contradiction: "despite the fact that there has been no increase of intermediate products and there is no possibility of lengthening the average period of production, a rate of interest is established in the loan market which corresponds to a longer period of production" [III.19.39]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Step 4, the wall: "A time must necessarily come when the means of subsistence available for consumption are all used up although the capital goods employed in production have not yet been transformed into consumption goods." [III.19.39]; "The means of subsistence will prove insufficient to maintain the laborers during the whole period of the process of production that has been entered upon." [III.19.39]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Step 5, relative prices reverse: "The market prices of consumption goods rise and those of production goods fall." [III.19.39]; "But soon a countermovement sets in: the prices of consumption goods rise, those of production goods fall. That is, the rate of interest on loans rises again, it again approaches the natural rate." [III.19.40]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Step 6, depreciation adds a second push: "so long as this depreciation of money is going on, the rate of interest on loans must rise above the level that would be demanded and paid if the objective exchange value of money remained unaltered" [III.19.41]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Step 7, doubling down fails: "However much the banks may endeavor to extend their credit circulation, they cannot stop the rise in the rate of interest." An unending issue "must lead to a fall in the objective exchange value of the money-and-credit unit to the paniclike course of which there can be no bounds" [III.19.42]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Step 8, the end and the loss: "Thus the banks will ultimately be forced to cease their endeavors to underbid the natural rate of interest." The recovery "involves certain of the phenomena of a crisis, which are signs of the loss of some of the capital invested in the excessively lengthened roundabout processes of production" (exact fragment: "signs of the loss of some of the capital invested in the excessively lengthened roundabout processes of production"); "Economic goods which could have satisfied more important wants have been employed for the satisfaction of less important" [III.19.43]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Worked example in III.19.43: an enterprise yielding four percent is founded when the loan rate falls from four and one-half to three and one-half percent, and becomes unprofitable when the rate returns to four and one-half. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)

#### How the boom ends (ch. 19 §5, III.19.44-45; [1924 rewrite])
- "Our theory of banking, like that of the currency principle, leads ultimately to a theory of business cycles." [III.19.44]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Historically banks stopped early: "They have always left off long before reaching this limit" and "And so the crises broke out before they need have broken out." [III.19.45]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Postponement only: "Certainly, the banks would be able to postpone the collapse; but nevertheless, as has been shown, the moment must eventually come when no further extension of the circulation of fiduciary media is possible." [III.19.45]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Severity scales with duration and distortion: "Then the catastrophe occurs, and its consequences are the worse and the reaction against the bull tendency of the market the stronger, the longer the period during which the rate of interest on loans has been below the natural rate of interest and the greater the extent to which roundabout processes of production that are not justified by the state of the capital market have been adopted." [III.19.45]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Later restatements in the prefaces: "Recurring economic crises are nothing but the consequence of attempts, despite all the teachings of experience and all the warnings of the economists, to stimulate economic activity by means of additional credit." [HP.15], Preface to the English Edition [1934]; the fall in prices of 1929-34 was "the inevitable and ineluctable consequence of the expansion of credit, which was bound to lead eventually to a collapse" [HP.13] [1934]; the demagogue "chooses inflation and credit expansion although he knows that the boom they create is short-lived and must inevitably end in a slump" (exact: "He chooses inflation and credit expansion although he knows that the boom they create is short-lived and must inevitably end in a slump.") [P.4], Preface to the New Edition [1952]. — [Econlib historical prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4); [Econlib 1952 preface](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=2)

#### Secondary reading and counter-evidence
- Hulsmann on what was new relative to Wicksell: "Strictly speaking, Wicksell had no explanation of the business cycle at all"; Mises's addition was that deviations between the two rates "entail an intertemporal misallocation of resources" (p. 253). — [Hulsmann p. 253](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf)
- Wikipedia's summary of the theory: "According to Mises and Hayek, an expansion of bank credit, not backed by prior savings, pushes the market rate of interest below the \"natural rate\" that would coordinate savings and investments." and "The Austrian business cycle theory has its roots in the 19th-century work of Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk on capital theory." It dates the first statement thus: "A similar theory appeared in the last few pages of Mises's The Theory of Money and Credit (1912)." — [Wikipedia, Austrian business cycle theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory)
- Counter-evidence, stated plainly on the same page: "According to John Quiggin, most economists believe that the Austrian business cycle theory is incorrect because of its incompleteness and other problems."; "In 1969, Nobel laureate Milton Friedman found the theory to be inconsistent with empirical evidence."; Sraffa (1932) objected that Hayek's version did not explain why "forced savings" induced by inflation would generate less sustainable investment; "Empirical economic research findings are inconclusive, with different economic schools of thought arriving at different conclusions." — [Wikipedia, Austrian business cycle theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory)

### Inferences
- In this book "the natural rate" is a capital-theoretic, in-kind rate: the rate at which the existing subsistence fund is just used up over the chosen period of production. It is not Wicksell's price-stabilising "normal rate" (which Mises treats separately at III.19.29), and it is not the modern estimated r-star. An essay sentence should say "the rate that real saving and real capital would set" or quote III.19.28, and should not write "the rate consistent with stable prices".
- Mises explicitly separates two things the essay must not blur: a fall in the natural rate itself (more real saving, a larger subsistence fund: III.19.16) and a money rate pushed below an unchanged natural rate (III.19.38, quoted under Key Question 3). The ageing literature the essay cites (ageing lowers returns through saving and capital deepening) is, in this book's vocabulary, a claim about the first; the crisis theory is only about the second. Nothing in Part III licenses reading every low interest rate as "artificial".
- Wikipedia's "last few pages" is loose for the edition the essay cites: the cycle argument is III.19.37-45, the end of ch. 19, with the long ch. 20 after it.
- One sentence in III.19.43 reads oddly: after the crisis "the only remaining trace of the disturbance will be a general increase in the objective exchange value of money due to factors emanating from the monetary side". The argument (III.19.41) implies a decrease in money's value. Probably a slip in translation or printing; do not quote that sentence.

### Gaps
- Wicksell's Geldzins und Guterpreise was not read; his definitions are as reported by Mises, Hulsmann and Wikipedia.
- Friedman's, Quiggin's and Sraffa's criticisms are cited from Wikipedia's summary, not from the original papers.
- No modern empirical test of the Mises 1912 mechanism was reviewed here.

## 3. Real saving versus credit creation as sources of loanable funds: can fiduciary media substitute for saving, and is "forced saving" discussed?

### Takeaway
Mises's answer is that banks can create loans but not capital: "The banks cannot evoke capital out of nothing." [III.17.30]. The words "forced saving" never occur in the book, but the mechanism does: issuing fiduciary media shifts wealth toward the issuers and borrowers, may raise saving, and to that extent can lower even the natural rate (a 1912 concession Mises hedges at once); the crisis theory concerns the further fall of the money rate that no such saving covers.

### Cited Findings
- Borrowers want capital: "Would-be borrowers are in search of capital, not money." [III.17.30], ch. 17 §6 [1912]; "it is never shortage of money in the proper sense of the word that drives them to present their bills for discounting" [III.17.31], ch. 17 §6. — [Econlib ch. 17](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=21)
- Banks cannot create it: "It is clear that this can only provide a provisional satisfaction of the demands for capital. The banks cannot evoke capital out of nothing." [III.17.30], ch. 17 §6. — [Econlib ch. 17](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=21)
- Who pays (the forced-saving mechanism without the name): "The cost of creating capital for borrowers of loans granted in fiduciary media is borne by those who are injured by the consequent variation in the objective exchange value of money" [III.17.30], ch. 17 §6. — [Econlib ch. 17](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=21)
- Fiduciary media come "practically out of nothing": "Since the issuer creates the present good that it surrenders in the exchange--the fiduciary media--practically out of nothing" [III.17.17], ch. 17 §4; "The quantity of fiduciary media in circulation has no natural limits." [III.17.26], ch. 17 §4. — [Econlib ch. 17](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=21)
- The classical question he inherits: "the only point at issue is whether the banks can grant credit beyond the available amount of capital or not" [III.19.6], ch. 19 §1. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Real saving does lower interest: "If the distribution of income and property is modified in such a way as to increase capacity for saving, then eventually the ratio between the value of present goods and future goods must be modified in favor of the latter." [III.19.16], ch. 19 §2; "The greater the fund of means of subsistence in a community, the lower the rate of interest." [III.19.16]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- The 1912 concession, redistribution toward issuers raises accumulation: "An increase in the stock of money in the broader sense caused by an issue of fiduciary media means a displacement of the social distribution of property in favor of the issuer." The resulting fall in the loan rate "must be in part permanent"; "There is a high degree of probability that extensive issues of fiduciary media by the banks represent a strong impulse toward the accumulation of capital and have consequently contributed to the fall in the rate of interest." [III.19.19], ch. 19 §3 [1912]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- The hedge, same section: the redistribution "can in no circumstances be very considerable" (exact: "it can in no circumstances be very considerable"), and "we do not intend to base any kind of further argument on such an undemonstrable proposition" [III.19.20], ch. 19 §3. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- The same concession inside the cycle argument: an increase of fiduciary media redistributes wealth in a way "which may well lead to increased saving and a reduction of the standard of living"; "So far as these factors enter into consideration, an increase of fiduciary media does cause a diminution of even the natural rate of interest, as we could show if it were necessary." [III.19.38], ch. 19 §4. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- The line he then draws: "We are not concerned with a reduction in the natural rate of interest brought about by an increase in the issue of fiduciary media, but with a reduction below this rate in the money rate charged by the banks" [III.19.38]. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Cheap credit weakens voluntary saving: "This time must come all the more quickly inasmuch as the fall in the rate of interest weakens the motive for saving and so slows up the rate of accumulation of capital." [III.19.39], ch. 19 §4. — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- The 1934 summary sentence: "expansion of credit cannot form a substitute for capital" (in context: "the conclusion to which my investigations lead, that expansion of credit cannot form a substitute for capital") [HP.16], Preface to the English Edition [1934]. — [Econlib historical prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4)
- Saving and world development, 1934: "The savings of the few advanced capitalistic countries of a small part of Europe have called into being the modern productive equipment of the whole world." [HP.12], Preface to the English Edition [1934]. — [Econlib historical prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4)
- "Forced saving": zero occurrences of the phrase in any file of the local text (all four Parts, prefaces, appendices). Hulsmann nonetheless describes the 1912 chapter as containing "the same discussion of forced savings" as later editions (p. 254 n. 83). — [Hulsmann p. 254](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf)
- Mises uses the term later and is sceptical: "It is true that such forced saving can originate from an inflationary movement and occasionally did so originate in the past." [4.XX.33]; "First one must realize that forced saving can result from inflation, but need not necessarily." [4.XX.34], Human Action ch. XX §5 (Econlib, 4th rev. ed.). — [Econlib, Human Action ch. XX](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/HmA/msHmA.html?chapter_num=23)
- The benefits he grants fiduciary media are monetary, not capital-creating: "Fiduciary media tap a lucrative source of revenue for their issuer; they enrich both the person that issues them and the community that employs them." [III.18.9], ch. 18 §3 [1912]; the argument at III.17.4 (ch. 17 §1) is that they spare the resources that would otherwise go into producing monetary metal. Selgin and White lean on exactly these passages. — [Econlib ch. 18](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=22); [Selgin and White 1996, p. 94](https://cdn.mises.org/rae9_2_5.pdf)

### Inferences
- A faithful one-sentence summary: in Mises's 1912 account credit creation can redistribute and thereby force some saving, but it cannot stand in for the subsistence fund that longer production requires; the gap between what the money rate signals and what has actually been saved is what the crisis closes.
- The 1912 text is less absolute than the 1934 slogan. III.19.19 and III.19.38 concede a real, partly permanent effect of bank issues on accumulation and on the natural rate. If the essay quotes HP.16 ("cannot form a substitute for capital"), it should date it 1934 and not present it as the whole of the 1912 argument.
- Analogy only, flagged as such for the essay's pay-as-you-go section: both fiduciary media and unfunded pension promises are claims issued beyond any fund set aside to meet them ("a fund that did not exist before the loans were granted", III.15.23). The identity fails on Mises's own criterion: a pay-as-you-go contributor does give up present goods, the claim is not a money substitute and creates no purchasing power, and Mises says nothing about pensions anywhere in Part III. The comparison can illustrate "claims on future output not matched by saved real resources"; it cannot be attributed to Mises.

### Gaps
- Whether Mises used a German equivalent of "forced saving" (erzwungenes Sparen) in 1912 or 1924 was not checked against the German text.
- Human Action was consulted only for the handful of paragraphs cited; its full treatment of forced saving (4.XX.32-38, 54-60, 112) was not analysed.

## 4. Chapter 20: the future of fiduciary media, internationally co-ordinated credit expansion, the gold-exchange standard, and reserves held as claims on foreign centres

### Takeaway
Mises's 1912 conclusion (quoted by himself in 1924) is that the only way to remove human influence from credit is "to suppress all further issue of fiduciary media" [III.20.79] by extending Peel's Act to deposits, and that the real brake on expansion is not law but the absence of "a centralized world bank or of uniform procedure" [III.20.10] among issuing banks; once banks co-ordinate, "there will no longer be any limit" [III.20.83]. In 1924 he adds that a gold-exchange standard whose reserves are foreign claims on gold "undermines the whole gold-standard system" [III.18.42] and hands the value of gold to "one government, namely, that of the United States" [III.20.42].

### Cited Findings

#### The future of fiduciary media
- The proposal [III.20.79], ch. 20 §13 [1912 text, quoted by Mises in 1924]: "Now it is obvious that the only way of eliminating human influence on the credit system is to suppress all further issue of fiduciary media. The basic conception of Peel's Act ought to be restated and more completely implemented than it was in the England of his time by including the issue of credit in the form of bank balances within the legislative prohibition." One of its stated grounds: "the pernicious consequences connected with a divergence between the natural and money rates of interest" [III.20.79]. — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)
- Mises introduces these paragraphs as self-quotation: "The first German edition of this work, published in 1912, concluded with an attempt at a glimpse into the future history of money and credit." [III.20.78], ch. 20 §13. — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)
- The prediction [III.20.83], ch. 20 §13 [1912 text, quoted in 1924]: "It carries within itself the germ of its own destruction; the development of the fiduciary medium must necessarily lead to its breakdown. Once common principles for their circulation-credit policy are agreed to by the different credit-issuing banks, or once the multiplicity of credit-issuing banks is replaced by a single world bank, there will no longer be any limit to the issue of fiduciary media." — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)
- 1924 restatement [III.20.58], ch. 20 §12: "An obvious further step is to close the gap that was not reckoned with in their theory and consequently not provided for in their policy by limiting the issue of fiduciary media in whatever form, not merely that of banknotes." - 1924 closing task [III.20.85], ch. 20 §13: "It will be a task for the future to erect safeguards against the inflationary misuse of the monetary system by the government and against the extension of the circulation of fiduciary media by the banks." - Why Peel's Act failed: its most serious error "lies in its failure to recognize the essential similarity of banknotes and bank deposits" (exact: "The most serious lies in its failure to recognize the essential similarity of banknotes and bank deposits.") [III.20.15], ch. 20 §3. - "Yet experience of state regulation of banks-of-issue has been incomparably more unfavorable than experience of uncontrolled private enterprise." #### The gold-exchange standard and reserves as foreign claims
- What the system is (ch. 16 §3, [1912] with light 1924 edits): "it was possible to create a gold standard without a gold currency" [III.16.22]; "it is customary to speak of it as the gold-exchange standard" and, against treating it as a different species, "the difference between the two systems is only one of degree, not one of kind" [III.16.25]. — [Econlib ch. 16](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=20)
- Reserves as foreign claims let more of the issue become fiduciary [III.18.41], ch. 18 §8: "if part of the redemption fund is invested in foreign banknotes, or in foreign bills, foreign checks, and deposits at short notice with foreign banks, then a larger proportion of the money substitutes issued by the banks can be transformed into fiduciary media". — [Econlib ch. 18](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=22)
- Origin and motive [III.18.42], ch. 18 §8: "so the central banks have taken the step of investing their stock of metal partly in foreign bills and other foreign credits"; "Generally speaking, it was the central banks-of-issue and the governmental redemption funds of the smaller and financially weaker countries that tried to save expense in this way. Since the war, which has made the whole world poorer, their procedure has been widely imitated." [second sentence 1924]. — [Econlib ch. 18](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=22)
- It cannot be generalised [III.18.42], ch. 18 §8 [1924]: "It is clear that the policy of investing the whole redemption fund in foreign claims to gold cannot become universal." "If all the countries of the world were to go over to the gold-exchange standard and hold their redemption funds not in gold but in foreign claims to gold, gold would no longer be required for monetary purposes at all." "The maintenance of a gold-exchange standard with the redemption fund invested in foreign bills undermines the whole gold-standard system." — [Econlib ch. 18](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=22)
- The pyramid of reserve centres [III.20.42], ch. 20 §9 [1924]: "Yet the gold-standard system was already undermined before the war." "The first step was the abolition of the physical use of gold in individual payments and the accumulation of the stocks of gold in the vaults of the great banks-of-issue." "The next step was the adoption of the practice by a series of states of holding the gold reserves of the central banks-of-issue (or the redemption funds that took their place), not in actual gold, but in various sorts of foreign claims to gold." The big banks of issue "became the central reserve banks of the world, as previously the central banks-of-issue had become central reserve banks for individual countries" (exact fragment begins "and so these banks became"). — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)
- Dependence on one issuer [III.20.42]: "Fluctuations in the price of gold are nowadays substantially dependent on the behavior of one government, namely, that of the United States." Batson's footnote 107 on the §9 heading: "[The reader will remember that this was written in 1924. H.E.B.]"; footnote 108 cites Keynes, A Tract on Monetary Reform (1923), pp. 163 ff. — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)
- What its advocates missed [III.20.43], ch. 20 §9 [1924]: "They have not observed that the gold-exchange standard must at last mean depriving gold of that characteristic which is the most important from the point of view of monetary policy--its independence of government influence upon fluctuations in its value." - Where it leads [III.20.44], ch. 20 §9: "If the gold-exchange standard is retained, the question must sooner or later arise as to whether it would not be better to substitute for it a credit-money standard whose fluctuations were more susceptible to control than those of gold." The alternative [III.20.45], ch. 20 §9: "an attempt must be made to get back from the gold-exchange standard to the actual use of gold again"; its one recommendation [III.20.47], ch. 20 §10: "the necessity for making a recurrence of inflationary policies if not impossible at least substantially more difficult" (exact fragment begins "this is the necessity"). — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)
- Verdict [III.20.77], ch. 20 §13 [1924]: "The gold-exchange standard might be considered as a possible basis for future currency systems only if an international agreement could impose upon each state the obligation to maintain a stock of gold of a size corresponding to its capacity. A gold-exchange standard with a redemption fund chiefly invested in foreign bills in gold currencies is in the long run not a practicable general solution of the problem." — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)
- Austria's own case [III.20.41], ch. 20 §9: "Thus Austria has a dollar-exchange standard and so, indirectly, a gold-exchange standard." The gold standard's merit: "it did preserve the monetary system from the influence of governments and changing policies" [III.20.41]. — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)

#### Capital-rich countries, capital export and low interest (the nearest Part III comes to the essay's current-account theme)
- France [III.20.20], ch. 20 §4 [1912]: "France is of all the countries in the whole world that which is richest in capital; but its people are not greatly endowed with the spirit of initiative and enterprise. Consequently its capital has to emigrate." "Now in a country which exports capital, even disregarding the premium for risk-bearing that is contained in the gross rate of interest, the rate of interest on loans must be lower than in a country which imports capital." "The cause is a general economic cause; it is a matter in which measures of banking or currency policy can have no influence." Even the Bank of France "was not exempt from the necessity for paying due attention to the level of the natural rate of interest" (exact: "In fixing the level of its discount rate, it was not exempt from the necessity for paying due attention to the level of the natural rate of interest."). — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)
- Imported shocks are normal [III.20.13], ch. 20 §3: "The fact that the rate of interest in Germany may rise, not because any change has occurred in its determinants within the Reich but because there have been changes, say, in the United States, should not seem any more remarkable than, say, a rise in the price of corn that is due to the state of foreign harvests." — [Econlib ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)

### Inferences
- Mises holds two positions that sit in tension, and the essay should not flatten them: the 1912 programme is legal suppression of new fiduciary media (Peel's Act extended to deposits); the 1924 addition distrusts state regulation, leans toward free banking with one prohibition, and still ends by calling for "safeguards" against bank expansion. Which of these is "Mises's view" is contested in the secondary literature (Selgin and White versus Salerno).
- His structural claim is about co-ordination: competition among issuers that must redeem in a common outside money is the brake; central-bank monopoly, a world bank, or tacit agreement removes it. That is a claim about institutional design, and it is dated: the "tacit agreement" passage is 1924 and Batson flagged it as such in 1934.
- His objection to reserves held as claims on foreign centres is specific: it economises on gold, lets more money substitutes become fiduciary, concentrates the world's reserve in a few banks, and makes the standard's value depend on the reserve-centre government. Any parallel with present-day reserve currencies or current-account imbalances is the essay's analogy, not Mises's statement; he was writing about claims convertible into gold.
- III.20.13 and III.20.20 are useful for the essay's section on ageing, saving and current accounts because Mises already treats interest as set in a world capital market and explains a low national rate by capital abundance and capital export. He gives the cause as capital-richness plus lack of enterprise plus home bias ("a series of psychological factors"), not demography. France's low fertility in that period is not mentioned by Mises and should not be read into him.

### Gaps
- Batson's footnotes 80 and 89 refer to an "editor's Introduction, pp. 21-22" and footnote 79 to pp. 23-31; Econlib states that the Rothbard foreword and Robbins introduction are "not available online", so those pages could not be read.
- White (1992), "Mises on Free Banking and Fractional Reserves", and Salerno's reply were not read directly (mises.org blocked); only Selgin and White (1996) was read in full text.
- Part IV (1953), where Mises returns to these proposals, is outside this brief.

## 5. Does Part III mention population, age structure, thrift, old age, or a society that saves more or less?

### Takeaway
No. Across chapters 15-20, Appendix B and all three prefaces there is no mention of age structure, old age, pensions, thrift, generations as savers, inheritance or birth rates; population appears once, as a determinant of the demand for money, and saving is tied only to the level and distribution of income.

### Inferences
- The essay can state a clean negative for Part III: Mises gives the natural rate a real anchor (saving, the subsistence fund) but says nothing on what age structure does to that anchor. Any link from ageing to saving to the natural rate is the modern literature's, laid on top of his framework.
- The honest bridge is conditional: if ageing changes aggregate real saving, then in the 1912 framework the natural rate itself moves (III.19.16), and that is categorically different from the bank-induced gap that produces the crisis (III.19.38). The later Mises would add that no general theorem connects capital abundance to the rate of interest at all (Human Action 4.XIX.29-31), which cuts against using him as an authority for "ageing lowers interest rates".
- III.19.15 is the closest thing to a "who saves" statement and it is about income, borrowed from Fisher. The essay could note that the life-cycle idea (saving varying with age) is absent from this 1912/1924 text.

### Gaps
- The negative covers Part III, Appendix B and the prefaces only. Parts I, II and IV were not searched for context (a whole-book count found "pension" in II.8, II.13 and IV.23 and no "old age", "thrift" or "birth rate" anywhere, but those passages were not read; other researchers cover them).
- Whether Mises links saving to age or generations in other works beyond the three Human Action chapters sampled (XVIII-XX, where "generations" appears only as inherited capital, e.g. 4.XVIII.44) was not investigated.

## 7. Where the argument rests on Bohm-Bawerk's capital theory, and where Mises later changed his view

### Takeaway
The whole of chapter 19 is built with Bohm-Bawerk's apparatus (subsistence fund, period of production, roundabout processes), and Mises says so in a footnote while declining to endorse that theory. In Human Action (1949) he replaces it with pure time preference ("originary interest"), denies that the productivity of roundabout methods explains interest, and, per Hulsmann, drops the barter-economy "natural rate" in favour of the money rate that would prevail without credit expansion.

### Cited Findings
- The disclaimer, footnote 56, attached to the sentence on the origin of interest in III.19.1 (ch. 19 §1): "The fact that I have followed the terminology and method of attack of Böhm-Bawerk's theory of interest throughout this chapter does not imply that I am an adherent of that theory or am able to regard it as a satisfactory solution of the problem. But the present work does not afford scope for the exposition of my own views on the problem of interest; that must be reserved for a special study, which I hope will appear in the not too distant future. In such circumstances I have had no alternative but to develop my argument on the basis of Böhm-Bawerk's theory." (Footnote text from the Econlib page; not in the local files.) — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)
- Same point in the 1924 preface: "My attitude toward the theory of interest is different today from what it was in 1911" and "I have been obliged to postpone any treatment of the problem of interest (which lies outside the theory of indirect exchange)" [HP.21]. — [Econlib historical prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4)
- Bohm-Bawerkian load-bearing points in ch. 19, each footnoted to Kapital und Kapitalzins: the subsistence fund lowers interest (III.19.16, fn 67: p. 622); the natural rate bounded by the marginal productivity of lengthening the period of production and by a subsistence fund just sufficient to pay wages over it (III.19.37, fn 75: pp. 611 ff.); diminishing increments from longer roundabout processes (III.19.37, fn 76: pp. 151 ff.); the crisis as subsistence running out before long processes mature (III.19.39). — [Econlib ch. 19](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=23)

### Inferences
- To say accurately what "the natural rate" means in this book: it is Wicksell's in-kind rate, cashed out with Bohm-Bawerk's subsistence-fund and period-of-production theory, used by an author who states in a footnote that he does not accept that theory as final. The essay should attribute the capital-theoretic content to "Mises, writing in Bohm-Bawerk's terms" and avoid "Mises's theory of interest", which properly names the later time-preference doctrine.
- Three later changes bear on the essay: (1) interest grounded in time preference, with productivity demoted; (2) explicit denial that more capital or more saving must lower the rate of interest (4.XIX.29-31); (3) the benchmark for the cycle theory becomes a counterfactual money rate without credit expansion, not a barter-economy natural rate. Each makes the mature Mises a weaker, not stronger, witness for a mechanical "more saving, lower natural rate" story.
- The cycle mechanism itself survives the change of interest theory (credit expansion without saving misleads investment), which is why it is safe to cite TMC for the mechanism while flagging that its 1912 vocabulary is borrowed.

### Gaps
- Whether footnote 56 dates from 1912 or was added in 1924 could not be established from the sources read. The 1924 preface says his attitude to interest changed after 1911 and that he postponed the subject "in preparing this as in preparing the first edition", which is compatible with either.
- The promised "special study" on interest: Hulsmann says Mises planned such a paper in the 1920s and only treated the subject in Nationalökonomie (1940); no separate study was located.
- Human Action was sampled (chs XIX and XX, selected paragraphs), not read through; Nationalökonomie was not consulted. The claim that the barter-economy natural rate is "untenable" for the later Mises is Hulsmann's interpretation, not a quotation from Mises.
- Bohm-Bawerk's Kapital und Kapitalzins was not read; the dependence is documented from Mises's own footnotes and Hulsmann.
