## 1. What are the latest available net international investment positions, in dollars and as a share of GDP, for Japan, Germany, China and the United States? Give source, date and vintage.

### Takeaway
At end-2025 the three ageing surplus economies each held net claims on the rest of the world of roughly $3.6-4.3 trillion: Germany EUR 3,661 billion (81% of GDP), China $4,071 billion (about 21% of GDP), Japan JPY 561.75 trillion (about 85% of GDP, my arithmetic). The United States owed the world a net $27.5 trillion at end-2025 (about 90% of GDP, my arithmetic) and $22.4 trillion at mid-2026; its position moves by trillions of dollars a quarter on valuation alone, so any single figure needs its date.

### Inferences
- Ratios I computed (not published as such): Japan 561.75 / 663.76 = about 85% of GDP. United States end-2025: 27.54 / 30.8 = about 90% of GDP. United States Q2 2026: 22.42 / about 32.5 (Q1 level grown one quarter at 8.0% annualised) = about 69% of GDP. The US GDP inputs are third-party extracts, so print these as 'about 90%' and 'about 70%', not to a decimal.
- Dollar equivalents at the exchange rate implied by the MOF ranking (SAFE's $4,071.3 billion = JPY 636.3 trillion, so about JPY 156.3 per dollar): Japan about $3.6 trillion, Germany about $4.3 trillion, China $4.07 trillion. Together about $12 trillion, a little under half of the US net liability at end-2025.
- The US net liability fell by about $6 trillion between end-2025 (-$27.54 trillion) and end-Q1 2026 (-$21.27 trillion), almost entirely through liabilities (from $70.49 trillion to roughly $64.5 trillion; the second figure is my subtraction from the Q2 release, and the two come from different data vintages). That is a price and exchange-rate effect, not repayment. It shows that creditors' claims on the United States are now largely equity-like and marked to market: the creditor's exposure is to US asset prices and the dollar at least as much as to US consumer-price inflation.
- 'In whose currency' differs by creditor. Japan and China hold claims largely on the dollar area and largely in dollars (reserves, Treasuries, US portfolio assets). Germany's position is different in kind: a little over a quarter of it (EUR 1,023 billion of EUR 3,661 billion, if the units are as inferred) is a TARGET claim in its own currency on its own central-bank system. A German saver's exposure is mainly to ECB policy, which Germany co-governs, not to the Federal Reserve.
- Rough scale of Japan's dollar exposure, as an illustration only: US-located portfolio investment and FDI ($3.29 trillion) are about JPY 514 trillion, about 77% of GDP; adding foreign-currency reserves gives an upper bound near 104% of GDP for these three items. A 10% fall of the dollar against the yen would then cost on the order of 8-10% of GDP before hedging. Location is not currency and part is hedged (question 4), so this is an order of magnitude, not a statistic.

## 2. What share of allocated official foreign-exchange reserves is held in US dollars, per the IMF COFER data, in the latest quarter available, and how has it moved since 2000?

### Takeaway
The dollar's share of allocated reserves was about 56.7% at end-June 2026 (latest), down from about 70-71% in 1999-2001: a loss of some 14 percentage points in a quarter of a century, most of it before 2010, with the dollar still nearly three times the euro's share. The 2022 freeze did not produce a visible break in the series; the post-2022 movement is small, partly valuation, and the real shift is into gold, which COFER does not count.

### Inferences
- Shape of the series: roughly 70-71% (1999-2001), a step down to about 65% in 2002, a long plateau of about 62-65% to 2016, then a slide to about 59% by 2020-2023 and about 57% in 2025-26. The fall between end-2021 (59.40%) and end-2024 (58.52%), the three years after the freeze, is under one percentage point; the larger 2025 move coincides with a falling dollar and is partly valuation.
- 'Lowest this century' is accurate; 'lowest ever' is not: the transcribed series shows 58.96% in 1995 and 47.14% in 1990.
- For the essay's question, COFER understates diversification in one way (gold is excluded, and gold's share has risen with its price) and overstates it in another (a falling dollar mechanically lowers the dollar share without any sale).

- The exchange-rate-adjusted (constant-currency) dollar share for 2025-26 was not retrieved.

## 3. How much gold have central banks bought each year since 2020 according to the World Gold Council, and which countries led? What amount of Russian central-bank reserves was immobilised in 2022 and where?

### Takeaway
Central-bank net gold purchases jumped from 255 tonnes in 2020 and about 450-463 tonnes in 2021 to more than 1,000 tonnes in each of 2022, 2023 and 2024, then 863 tonnes in 2025 and 675 tonnes in the first three quarters of 2026; well over half of recent totals is unreported buying that the World Gold Council estimates rather than observes. About $300 billion of Russia's roughly $612-630 billion of reserves was immobilised on 28 February 2022, about EUR 210 billion of it in the EU and most of that at Euroclear in Belgium; in December 2025 the EU made the freeze indefinite but did not confiscate.

## 5. What do Gourinchas & Rey (2007, 'From world banker to world venture capitalist'), Gourinchas, Rey & Govillot ('Exorbitant privilege and exorbitant duty') and the Triffin dilemma literature say about the issuer's gains and the creditor's exposure, including valuation losses imposed on creditors by dollar depreciation or inflation?

### Takeaway
The literature agrees that the issuer earns more on its foreign assets than it pays on its liabilities and that a falling dollar transfers wealth from the rest of the world to the United States; it also finds that in crises the transfer runs the other way (about 20% of US GDP in 2007-09), that since about 2007 foreigners have gained heavily on US equities, and that the 'privilege' may have ended. The creditor's exposure is real but two-sided, and it runs through exchange rates and asset prices more than through consumer-price inflation alone.

### Inferences
- Three distinct channels expose a creditor to the issuer, and the literature treats them separately: (a) exchange-rate valuation (Tille; Gourinchas & Rey; BIS WP 684), (b) erosion of nominal claims by the issuer's inflation and, late in a hegemony, financial repression (Chen et al.; Hilscher, Raviv & Reis on its limits), (c) the issuer's low funding cost, which is the creditor's low return (the 'return discount'). Channel (a) is the best measured; channel (b) is the one closest to the essay's Mises theme and the least well quantified for foreign holders.
- The direction of the valuation transfer has reversed more than once: toward the US during the dollar's fall of 2002-11; toward the rest of the world in 2007-09 (about 20% of US GDP) and through the US equity boom since (Atkeson et al.); toward the US again in the dollar's slide of April-May 2025. A sentence saying creditors 'lose' to the issuer needs a period attached.
- The composition of creditor claims has shifted from bonds toward equity and direct investment (BIS: foreigners held $17.6 trillion of US equities against $13.6 trillion of US bonds in March 2025). Equity is a claim on real earnings and is less exposed to consumer-price inflation than a nominal bond, but fully exposed to the exchange rate and to US valuations. The pure 'inflate away the foreigners' story applies to the roughly one-third of Treasuries held abroad, not to the whole net position.
- The Mises passage is a fair antecedent for one point only: when reserves are held as claims on a centre country, their value depends on that country's policy. In 1924 the claim was a claim to gold and the policy was the Federal Reserve's gold absorption; today the claim is a fiat dollar. The essay should present it as an analogy of structure.
- Hünnekes, Schularick & Trebesch supply a point the ageing essay can use directly: an old creditor that lends mainly to other old economies has not diversified its demographic risk, whatever the currency.

## 6. What does the security literature say about ageing and power - Mark Haas, 'A Geriatric Peace?' (International Security, 2007), Brooks, Brooks, Greenhill & Haas (2019), Eberstadt, and the essay's own target, Peter Zeihan - in particular on pension and health costs crowding out defence?

### Takeaway
Haas (2007) argues that ageing will crowd out military spending in every great power, extending US primacy because the United States ages least, while also shrinking what the United States itself can afford; Brooks, Brooks, Greenhill & Haas (2019) find that interstate conflict onset falls sharply as societies age. ## 7. Historically, how have large wars and large debts been financed through money creation (the First World War inflations, post-1945 financial repression)? Cite standard sources, briefly.

### Takeaway
The belligerents of 1914-18 covered at most about a quarter of war spending by taxes (Britain, the United States) and far less elsewhere (Germany and Italy 6-15%, Austria nil), borrowing the rest increasingly from their central banks; Germany's war debt of 156 billion marks ended in the 1923 hyperinflation. After 1945 the victors worked debt down by a milder method: interest-rate ceilings plus inflation, worth an estimated 3-4% of GDP a year to the US and UK treasuries.

### Cited Findings
- "During World War I, the United States and Great Britain each financed one quarter of their war costs via increased taxation, while in Austria the contribution of taxation towards the expenditure was zero." `[opened]` — [Wikipedia, War finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_finance)
- In Germany and Italy between 6 and 15% of war spending in real terms was financed from taxes; short-term 'floating' debt was placed largely with central and private banks `[snippet]` — [1914-1918-online, International Encyclopedia of the First World War, 'War Finance'](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/war_finance)
- Germany: "German Emperor Wilhelm II and the Reichstag decided unanimously to fund the war entirely by borrowing rather than risk angering the public with new taxes." `[opened]`; "the national debt stood at 156 billion marks in 1918" `[opened]`; by November 1923 one dollar was worth 4,210,500,000,000 marks `[opened]` — [Wikipedia, Hyperinflation in the Weimar Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation_in_the_Weimar_Republic)
- Keynes in 1919 (The Economic Consequences of the Peace), as quoted there: "The various belligerent Governments, unable, or too timid or too short-sighted to secure from loans or taxes the resources they required, have printed notes for the balance." `[opened: Wikipedia's quotation; Keynes's page not located]` — [Wikipedia, Hyperinflation in the Weimar Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation_in_the_Weimar_Republic)
- Standard comparative study: T. Balderston, 'War Finance and Inflation in Britain and Germany, 1914-1918', Economic History Review 42(2), 1989, pp. 222-244 `[snippet: citation only]` — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:ehsrev:v:42:y:1989:i:2:p:222-244)
- United States: Hall & Sargent, 'Three World Wars: Fiscal-Monetary Consequences', Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 119 (2022), e2200349119 `[snippet]`: the two world wars and the 'war on COVID-19' were all financed chiefly by interest-bearing debt and base money, with taxes rising far less than spending, and the Federal Reserve supported the Treasury market each time — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:119:y:2022:p:e2200349119); [Hoover Institution PDF](https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/hall_sargent_three_world_wars_pnas.pdf)
- Post-1945: Reinhart & Sbrancia, 'The Liquidation of Government Debt', Economic Policy 30(82), 2015, pp. 291-333 (NBER Working Paper 16893, 2011) `[snippet]`: real interest rates in advanced economies were negative about half the time in 1945-80; the annual 'liquidation effect' for the United States and United Kingdom averaged 3-4% of GDP; around 5% for Australia and Italy; interest savings of about 1-5% of GDP a year across a twelve-country sample — [NBER w16893](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16893); [Economic Policy listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:ecpoli:v:30:y:2015:i:82:p:291-333.); [PIIE summary](https://www.piie.com/publications/working-papers/liquidation-government-debt)
- The mechanism in one line: "financial repression is most successful in liquidating debts when accompanied by inflation" `[opened]` — [Wikipedia, Financial repression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_repression)
- The international version, 1965-71: Bretton Woods could not survive "once the US began following inflationary policies in the mid-1960s" `[opened, p. 7]` — [BIS Working Papers No 684](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-684-triffin-dilemma-or-myth.pdf)
- Mises's own reading of pre-1914 policy, for the essay's primary text: etatism restricted gold circulation "second, because it wished to accumulate a war chest; and third, because it wished to wean the people from the use of gold coins so as to pave the way for the inflationary policy of the coming Great War." [III.20.47], Part III, chapter 20 'Problems of Credit Policy', section '10 A Return to a Gold Currency' `[opened: local text, exact match]` — [Econlib, chapter 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)

### Inferences
- The two historical methods map onto different creditors. Open inflation (Germany 1914-23) destroyed domestic nominal savers. Financial repression (1945-80) required a captive domestic market, which ceilings and capital controls supplied; foreign holders can leave. The Bretton Woods episode is the one in which the issuer's inflation was passed to foreign official creditors, and it ended in a unilateral change of terms (August 1971), not in repayment at the old value.
- The post-1945 liquidation estimates are for domestic debt under controls; applying '3-4% of GDP a year' to today's foreign holders of dollar claims would pass an analogy off as identity. The sibling notes file ageing_interest_inflation_pensions.md (section 5) covers Reinhart & Sbrancia and fiscal dominance in more detail.

### Gaps
- Balderston's own tax-share figures and Hall & Sargent's breakdown of financing by taxes, debt and money for each war were not in the extracts.
- The Keynes sentence is quoted from Wikipedia; chapter and page in The Economic Consequences of the Peace should be located before printing.
- Wikipedia's 'one quarter' for the United States and Britain is unsourced in the extract I read; the 1914-1918-online encyclopedia gives 'at most about a quarter', which is the safer wording.

### Inferences
- A defensible one-sentence answer to the essay's question, with its limits: an old creditor nation holds a large part of its retirement saving as claims on younger or more indebted issuers (Japan and China mostly in dollars, Germany mostly in euros on its own union); the value of those claims in the creditor's own consumption basket depends on the issuer's exchange rate, asset prices and, for the bond portion, inflation; the record shows transfers in both directions, with the creditor losing in 1965-73, 2002-11 and April-May 2025 and gaining in 2007-09 and 2021-24.
- The strongest evidence against a simple 'creditors are at the issuer's mercy' reading: (1) Japan's net external assets reached a record in yen in every recent year, helped by a weak yen; (2) foreigners' gains on US equities are the main reason the US net liability tripled relative to GDP after 2007 (Atkeson et al.); (3) inflating away debt is hard at short maturities without repression (Hilscher, Raviv & Reis); (4) no visible flight from the dollar in COFER after 2022.
- The strongest evidence for it: (1) the BIS account of unhedged Asian investors' losses in April 2025; (2) the Bretton Woods precedent, in which the issuer's inflation was exported and the terms were changed unilaterally in 1971; (3) Chen et al.'s finding that earlier hegemonies ended with bondholder losses and financial repression; (4) the 2022 freeze as proof that such claims are politically conditional, with the caveat that this is a different risk.
- For a 250-400 word section, the figures that can carry weight without long qualification are: the four NIIP numbers with dates; 'about 57% of allocated reserves, down from about 71% at the turn of the century'; 'about $300 billion immobilised, most of it in Belgium'; 'foreigners hold about a third of US Treasuries'; and the life-insurer hedge ratio. Everything else needs a qualifier or should stay in a note.
