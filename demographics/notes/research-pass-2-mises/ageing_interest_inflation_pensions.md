## 1. State the classic result that an unfunded pension returns the growth rate of the wage bill (population growth plus productivity growth): Samuelson (1958) and Aaron (1966). Give full citations and the exact form of the result.

### Takeaway
A pay-as-you-go scheme with a fixed contribution rate pays each cohort an implicit real return equal to the growth of the contribution base: population growth n in Samuelson's pure consumption-loan model, n + g (population growth plus real-wage growth) in Aaron's version; it beats a funded scheme only if n + g > r. The result is a statement about real returns and contains no price level: inflation enters only through indexation rules.

### Cited Findings
- **Samuelson (1958).** Paul A. Samuelson, "An Exact Consumption-Loan Model of Interest with or without the Social Contrivance of Money," *Journal of Political Economy* 66(6), December 1958, pp. 467–482, DOI 10.1086/258100. Citation (volume, issue, pages) confirmed from the JPE listing `[snippet]`. The search extract confirms it is the origin of the overlapping-generations framework and that Samuelson "suggested the social contrivance of money as a solution" to the model's inefficiency `[snippet]` — [JPE / University of Chicago Press](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/258100)
- Wikipedia's account of the OLG model: it "was first formulated in 1947, in the context of a pure-exchange economy, by Maurice Allais, and more rigorously by Paul Samuelson in 1958. In 1965, Peter Diamond incorporated an aggregate neoclassical production into the model" `[opened]` — [Wikipedia, Overlapping generations model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overlapping_generations_model)
- **Aaron (1966), exact statement.** Henry Aaron, "The Social Insurance Paradox," *Canadian Journal of Economics and Political Science* 32(3), August 1966, pp. 371–374. The result in Aaron's words, as quoted in the Introduction of Drees (2025): "social insurance can increase the welfare of each person if the sum of the rates of growth of population and real wages exceeds the rate of interest" `[opened: the quoting paper; Aaron's own page not opened]` — [Drees, "Resolving Aaron's Social Insurance Paradox," arXiv:2504.00909 (2025), §1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00909)

- Drees (2025) on where Samuelson fits: "In settings where public debt is not possible, a PAYG pension system indeed offers an advantage: it effectively functions as a mechanism for taking on debt. This setting is also the one initially explored by Samuelson (1958)." He argues the Aaron advantage "stems from asset reduction rather than inherent superiority" (abstract) `[opened]`. This is one unrefereed arXiv paper, useful for the quotation and as a statement of the PAYG-equals-implicit-debt view, not as settled literature — [arXiv:2504.00909](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00909)
- Institutional echo: OECD *Pensions at a Glance 2025* describes automatic adjustment mechanisms "adapting pensions to total contributions or a proxy thereof, such as growth of the wage bill, GDP or the number of contributors" `[snippet]` — [OECD, Pensions at a Glance 2025, "Recent pension reforms"](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2025_e40274c1-en/full-report/recent-pension-reforms_146d2687.html)

### Inferences
- **Exact form (standard two-period derivation, not a quotation).** Let N_t workers each earn w_t and pay contribution rate τ, with each year's contributions paid out to that year's retirees. A member of cohort t pays τ·w_t when young and receives τ·w_{t+1}·N_{t+1}/N_t when old. The implicit gross return is (w_{t+1}/w_t)·(N_{t+1}/N_t) = (1+g)(1+n), so the net return is n + g + n·g ≈ n + g: the growth rate of the wage bill. Samuelson's 1958 economy has no productivity growth, so the return is n alone; Aaron adds g and states the welfare condition n + g > r.
- **Mapping to the essay's section 10.** "Benefits held": the budget constraint τ_t·w_t·N_t = ρ·w_t·R_t gives τ_t = ρ / (N_t/R_t), i.e. contribution rate = replacement rate ÷ workers per retiree. "Contributions held": the scheme's return is the growth of the contribution base; measured net of wage growth it is n, which is exactly Samuelson's case. The essay's wording ("the scheme's return is the growth of the contribution base, net of wage growth") is consistent with both papers.
- The theorem is in real terms. A falling or negative n lowers the real return whatever the price level does. Inflation cannot repair the arithmetic; it can only redistribute who bears the shortfall, and only to the extent that claims are not indexed (question 4).
- Because every calibrated model in question 2 has ageing lowering r as well as n, the Aaron comparison (n + g against r) does not move one-for-one against PAYG as populations age; this is an inference, not a finding of any paper cited here.

## 2. What do the main papers find about demographics and the natural rate? Cover Carvalho, Ferrero & Nechio (2016); Eggertsson, Mehrotra & Robbins (2019); Gagnon, Johannsen & Lopez-Salido (2021); Lisack, Sajedi & Thwaites; Rachel & Summers (2019); Auclert, Malmberg, Martenet & Rognlie (2021/2024); and the opposing 'great demographic reversal' of Goodhart & Pradhan (2020). Give each headline number and its sign.

### Takeaway
Every calibrated life-cycle model in the list has the same sign: ageing has lowered the natural/equilibrium real rate, by roughly 1¼ to 1½ percentage points since 1980–90 (more in Eggertsson et al.), and will lower it further (Auclert et al.: −123 bp by 2100). Goodhart & Pradhan predict the opposite sign but offer no calibrated number, and their argument rests on a stated political assumption that the pension and health safety net stays in place.

### Inferences
- **Which side is better supported.** Five independently calibrated overlapping-generations models (Carvalho et al., Eggertsson et al., Gagnon et al., Lisack et al., Auclert et al.) and one panel VAR (Aksoy et al.) agree on sign and broadly on size. Goodhart & Pradhan is a reasoned extrapolation without a quantified model, and it depends on (i) retirees dissaving heavily, (ii) the safety net being kept, and (iii) the China labour-supply effect reversing. On the evidence available the "ageing lowers r*" side is better supported; the essay's existing treatment in §4 (Auclert et al., −123 bp) is in line with the rest of the literature.
- The two camps disagree less than the headlines suggest: all accept that a higher dependency ratio pushes r up and longer lives push it down (Carvalho's three channels; Juselius & Takáts p. 3). The dispute is which dominates in the coming decades.
- Relevance to pensions: Eggertsson et al. and Rachel & Summers both find that government debt, and in Rachel & Summers's case pay-as-you-go social insurance, has *raised* the neutral rate (about +2 pp from debt in Eggertsson et al.). Unfunded promises therefore show up in this literature as upward pressure on real rates, not as a cause of low rates. Cutting them would lower r* further.
- From the secondary figures, demographics alone (mortality + fertility) account for about −3.6 pp in Eggertsson et al.; this is my arithmetic on snippet values (1.8 + 1.8), not a number stated in the paper.

## 3. What does the evidence say on ageing and inflation? Cover Juselius & Takats (BIS Working Papers 485 and 722), Bullard, Garriga & Waller (2012), Katagiri, Konishi & Ueda, Bobeica, Lis, Nickel & Sun (ECB), Aksoy, Basso, Smith & Grasl (2019), and Japan's experience since the 1990s. Who finds the old are disinflationary, who the reverse, and why?

### Takeaway
The evidence is genuinely split and the split has a pattern. Studies that look only at the old-age share in post-war data (IMF work on Japan, Bobeica et al., Bullard et al.'s theory) find ageing disinflationary; studies that model the whole age distribution (Juselius & Takáts, Aksoy et al.) find dependants, young and old, inflationary and workers disinflationary, with the very old (80+) turning disinflationary again. Japan, the oldest large economy, had falling prices for fifteen years, which Juselius & Takáts attribute to fewer young people rather than more old ones.

### Inferences
- **Who finds what, and why.** Old disinflationary: Shirakawa (lower growth expectations, zero bound); Anderson et al. and Yoon et al. at the IMF (slower growth; panel regression); Bobeica et al. (working-age share cointegrated with core inflation); Bullard et al. (retirees are creditors and vote for low inflation); Katagiri et al. *when ageing comes from longevity*. Old inflationary: Juselius & Takáts for ages 65–79 (dependants consume without producing; rise in the dependency ratio raises the natural rate and policy lags); Aksoy et al. (as classified by Juselius & Takáts); Goodhart & Pradhan (labour scarcity plus taxes to fund transfers); Katagiri et al. *when ageing comes from low births*. The sign therefore turns on four things: which old (65–79 or 80+), which source of ageing (longevity or fertility), which sample (post-war only or 1870–2016), and whether monetary policy is constrained.
- **Which is better supported.** Neither sign is established. The Juselius–Takáts result is the most robust reduced-form finding (long panel, many specifications), but its forecast of roughly +3 pp of inflationary pressure rests on extrapolating to age structures with few precedents, the authors flag the very old as their least precise estimate, and in the long panel the 80+ coefficient is "sharply negative". The observed record of the oldest country, Japan, runs against a simple "old means inflation" reading; Juselius & Takáts reconcile it by pointing to the shrinking young share, which is an interpretation of fitted effects rather than an independent test.
- None of these papers describes inflation as a way of *defaulting* on pension promises. In Goodhart & Pradhan inflation arises because the promises are *honoured* and workers resist the taxes; in Katagiri et al. longevity produces *deflation* because bond-holding older generations gain political weight. This matters for the essay's thesis: the "ageing is inflationary" literature and the "promises get inflated away" claim are different claims.

## 4. How are public pensions in payment indexed in the OECD (prices, wages, mixed)? This decides whether inflation can erode them at all. Cite OECD Pensions at a Glance with edition and year. Which claims held by retirees are NOT indexed (nominal bonds, level annuities, cash, many private defined-benefit pensions)?

### Takeaway
More than half of OECD countries index public pensions in payment so as to protect them fully against inflation over time (prices, or prices plus some real-wage growth); most of the rest use a price–wage mix or wages. So sustained inflation cannot, by itself, erode most public pensions; what it erodes is retirees' *nominal* claims — bonds, cash, level annuities, capped or unindexed occupational pensions — plus, temporarily, pensions where indexation lags, is capped, or is deliberately set below prices.

### Inferences
- A working classification for the essay. *Indexed, so not erodible by inflation except through lags or rule changes:* public pensions in payment in most OECD countries. *Partly exposed:* pensions on mixed or wage indexation when real wages fall; pensions with caps (UK private DB), thresholds, multi-year lags, or a built-in below-price rule (Japan's slide). *Fully exposed:* nominal government and corporate bonds, bank deposits and cash, level (non-escalating) annuities, defined-contribution pots invested in nominal bonds, and defined-benefit pensions without statutory indexation (UK pre-1997 accruals; most US private-sector plans, see Gaps).
- So an inflation that "settles" unfunded promises mostly does not fall on the unfunded public pension itself. It falls on the *funded* and *private* part of retirement provision and on the government's nominal creditors. The public pension is cut by changing the indexation rule, which is a legislated act, visible and contestable, and not a monetary one.
- Japan's rule is the instructive hybrid: a legislated real cut that only operates when prices and wages are rising, because of the nominal floor. Inflation there is the enabling condition for a pension cut that deflation had blocked between 2004 and 2015.

## 5. What is known about inflation and financial repression as debt liquidation - Reinhart & Sbrancia (2015), Sargent & Wallace (1981), and recent work on fiscal dominance in ageing economies? Any estimates of implicit (unfunded) pension liabilities relative to GDP, with source and date?

### Takeaway
Post-war governments did liquidate debt with a mix of interest-rate controls and moderate, chronic inflation, worth about 2–3% of GDP a year in the US and UK from 1945 to 1980; but the mechanism works on nominal debt held by captive savers, not on indexed pension promises, and modern studies find it would do little for the US today without renewed repression. Accrued pension entitlements under social insurance are typically two to four times GDP in European countries, far larger than explicit debt, and they are overwhelmingly indexed.

- **What liquidation can and cannot reach.** The Reinhart–Sbrancia tax base is nominal government debt held by captive domestic institutions. It can shrink explicit debt by a few percent of GDP a year. It cannot shrink a price-indexed pension entitlement at all. Since accrued social-insurance entitlements in Italy, Sweden, Czechia or Norway are three to four times GDP, against explicit debt of roughly one to one and a half times GDP in the most indebted of them, the larger part of the "unfunded promise" is out of inflation's reach by construction. (The explicit-debt comparison is general knowledge, not sourced here.)
- The two-step version of the thesis is the defensible one: unfunded age-related spending raises deficits (BIS: +4% of GDP over 20 years in advanced economies); deficits become nominal debt; nominal debt can be liquidated by repression plus chronic inflation. On this reading inflation settles the *borrowing done to keep* the promises, not the promises.
- The burden of that settlement falls on savers, and so largely on retirees and their pension funds (Doepke & Schneider; Reinhart & Sbrancia on pension funds as the captive audience). An ageing society that inflates is therefore taxing its own old, which is the core of the political-economy objection in question 6.
- Entitlement measures are not comparable to each other: Eurostat's accrued-to-date liability is a closed-group stock (rights already earned), while the US Trustees' unfunded obligation is an open-group 75-year shortfall net of future contributions. Italy's 434% and the US 1.5% of GDP are different concepts and should never be set side by side as if alike.
- Sargent & Wallace's logic, applied here (my application): if the fiscal authority's path of primary deficits is fixed by indexed entitlements and the real rate exceeds growth, the central bank must eventually supply seigniorage. The BIS projections assume the opposite configuration (r one point below g) and still get debt above 200% of GDP, which is why the BIS calls them "rather optimistic".

## 6. Is there political-economy evidence that older electorates prefer low inflation (older voters as net nominal creditors)? Cover Bullard et al. and any empirical tests.

### Takeaway
Yes, with one important dissent. The balance-sheet premise is documented (the old are the main nominal creditors), the theory is explicit (Bullard, Garriga & Waller), and one political-science study finds older people more inflation-averse and older countries lower-inflation; but the longest panel study (Juselius & Takáts) rejects the reduced-form prediction, finding 65–79-year-olds associated with higher inflation.

### Cited Findings
- **Theory.** Bullard, Garriga & Waller (2012), *Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis Review* 94(6), pp. 419–439: young cohorts, with no assets and wage income, prefer "relatively high rates of inflation"; older generations "prefer higher rates of return from their savings, relatively low wages, and relatively low inflation"; when older cohorts have more influence over redistributive policy the economy has "a relatively low steady-state rate of inflation" `[snippet: abstract]` — [Fed in Print record](https://fedinprint.org/item/fedlrv/24667)
- Juselius & Takáts's gloss on the channel (BIS WP 722, p. 3): "the young are often borrowers and therefore prefer inflation, whereas the opposite holds for the old" `[opened]` — [BIS WP 722](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-722-enduring-link-between-demography-and-inflation.pdf)
- **The balance-sheet premise.** Doepke & Schneider (2006), *JPE* 114(6): 1069–1097: the main losers from inflation are "rich, old households, the major bondholders in the economy"; the main winners "young, middle-class households with fixed-rate mortgage debt" `[snippet]` — [EconPapers, JPE 114(6):1069–1097](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:v:114:y:2006:i:6:p:1069-1097)
- **Empirical test in favour.** Tim Vlandas, "Grey Power and the Economy: Aging and Inflation Across Advanced Economies," *Comparative Political Studies* 51(4), 2018, pp. 514–552, DOI 10.1177/0010414017710261. Abstract: countries with a larger share of elderly have lower inflation "because older people are both more inflation averse and politically powerful, forcing parties seeking their votes to pursue lower inflation"; survey logistic regressions show older people more inflation-averse and more likely to punish incumbents for inflation; social-democratic parties in older European countries publish more economically orthodox manifestos; the elderly share is negatively correlated with inflation in 21 OECD economies and in a wider sample of 175 countries `[snippet: abstract]` — [SAGE, Comparative Political Studies](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0010414017710261)
- **Empirical test against.** Juselius & Takáts (BIS WP 485, p. 35): "Our findings also suggest that demography affects inflation more through economic than political economy channels." The reasons given on p. 2: demography affects inflation even after controlling for real interest rates, and "country-specific demographic impacts are apparent in small euro area countries in spite of centralised policy rate setting by the European Central Bank" `[opened]` — [BIS WP 485](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-485-can-demography-affect-inflation-and-monetary-policy.pdf)
- Stronger still (BIS WP 722, p. 19): "A political economy explanation, where the median voter’s age-based preferences drive the inflation target, as detailed in Bullard et al (2012), does not seem to fit with the evidence either, because its effects are the opposite to what we find: the elderly, except for the very old, are inflationary not disinflationary." `[opened]` — [BIS WP 722](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-722-enduring-link-between-demography-and-inflation.pdf)
- **A politico-economic model with the Bullard sign.** Katagiri, Konishi & Ueda (2020) reach deflation from longevity through the "policy response of future governments" in a model where short-lived governments choose taxes and bond issues `[snippet]` — [EconPapers, JME 111:1–15](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:moneco:v:111:y:2020:i:c:p:1-15)
- **Grey power also protects the pension rules themselves.** OECD *Pensions at a Glance 2021* (December 2021): automatic adjustment mechanisms "were sometimes suspended or even eliminated in order to avoid pension benefit cuts and retirement-age increases"; "policy makers maintain full control over pensions and can intervene if they deem the triggered adjustments undesirable" `[snippet]` — [OECD Pensions at a Glance 2021](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2021_ca401ebd-en.html)
- Goodhart & Pradhan expect "a political confrontation between the elder cohorts" and workers over the safety net (BIS WP 656, pp. 1–2 and §3) `[opened; the quoted words are from the introduction's road-map sentence]` — [BIS WP 656](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-656-demographics-will-reverse-three-multi-decade-global-trends.pdf)

### Inferences
- The preference claim (older voters dislike inflation) is better supported than the outcome claim (older countries get less inflation). Vlandas supports both; Juselius & Takáts contradict the second for the under-80s. A fair statement: older electorates have a documented interest in low inflation and some evidence of acting on it, but it has not been shown to dominate the economics of the age structure.
- For the essay's thesis the political economy cuts against inflation as the chosen route, because the losers from inflation (nominal creditors) are the growing voting bloc. It also cuts against explicit cuts to current pensioners. What is left is the pattern the OECD records: adjustments loaded onto future retirees (retirement ages for cohorts not yet retired) and workers (contributions), with automatic mechanisms overridden when they would cut current benefits.
- Bullard et al. is a model, not a test, and its authors offered Japan as suggestive, not as proof.

## 7. What is the single strongest piece of evidence AGAINST the claim that ageing societies inflate their promises away (Japan is the obvious case)? And the strongest for it?

### Takeaway
Against: Japan, the oldest large economy with the highest public debt, saw consumer prices *fall* 4.1% over fiscal 1998–2012, and it adjusted its pensions by an explicit legislated formula that could not even operate under deflation; more generally the recorded settlement of pension promises across the OECD is by rule changes (later retirement, automatic adjustment, indexation changes, nominal cuts in Greece), on claims that are mostly price-indexed. For: the 1945–1980 liquidation of war debt by repression and chronic inflation (2.3% of GDP a year in the US, 3.0% in the UK), and its small-scale echo since 2022, above all in Japan, where debt has fallen about 22 points of GDP while pensions have risen by less than prices three years running.

### Inferences
- **Verdict on the reading "unfunded promises get settled by inflation".** As a description of the past it is wrong for pensions and right for one class of public debt. Pension promises have been settled by legislated changes to retirement ages, accrual and indexation, and in one euro-area case by nominal cuts. War debts were settled in good part by repression with chronic inflation. As a forecast it is unproven: it requires that ageing-driven deficits be financed by nominal debt and that central banks then accommodate, against the interest of an older electorate. Japan from 1995 to 2021 shows that neither step is automatic.
- **The strongest single fact against** is the Japanese price level: the society furthest along the path had deflation, not inflation, for fifteen years. **The strongest single fact for** is Reinhart & Sbrancia's Table 5 together with Acalin & Ball: within living memory, rich democracies did liquidate very large debts through negative real rates, and about 40 points of US debt/GDP went that way. The honest connection between the two is that the second concerned a one-off war debt in a young, fast-growing population under capital controls, while the first concerns a permanent, indexed flow obligation in an old one.
- **The nuance worth one sentence in the essay.** Japan since 2022 shows the hybrid at work: moderate inflation with policy rates held below it has reduced the debt ratio by about 22 points (my subtraction, 229 − 207), and has let a legislated pension formula deliver real cuts that deflation had blocked. Inflation there is not a substitute for rule changes but the condition under which the rule bites. It was also not obviously chosen: it arrived with the global 2021–23 surge and a weak yen.
- **Who pays.** In every documented inflationary settlement the loss falls on holders of nominal claims, who are disproportionately the old and their pension funds, while the indexed public pension is protected. "Inflating away promises to the old" therefore mostly means taxing the old's *savings* to keep paying the old's *pensions*; the claim is better stated as a transfer between kinds of retirement claim than as a default on the pension.
- **For the Mises section.** Any link to Mises's account of inflation as a concealed transfer from creditors to debtors is an analogy to this financial-repression literature, not a finding of it. None of the authors above cites Mises, and the modern results (indexation rules, r*, option-implied inflation risk, entitlement accounting) have no counterpart in a 1912/1924/1953 text. The parts of the modern evidence that sit *badly* with a simple inflationist reading should be stated: indexed pensions, Japan's deflation, older voters as creditors, and Hilscher et al.'s arithmetic.
