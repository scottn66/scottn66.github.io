## 1. How was the book received? Keynes's 1914 review, Wicksell's review, Robbins's 1934 introduction, Hayek's and Hicks's assessments, Rothbard's 1981 foreword

### Takeaway
The book was the standard German-language monetary text for two decades but reached English readers late (1934) and mostly through Hayek; the two contemporary reviews by first-rank economists were unfavourable (Keynes: "critical rather than constructive, dialectical and not original") or critical (Wicksell), the two prefatory essays printed with the English editions are advocacy (Robbins 1934, Rothbard 1981), and the most balanced scholarly verdict found is Moss (1976): "all the earmarks of a genuine economic classic" but "it lacks an acceptable methodological framework". Even Hayek, who called Mises's work the framework of his own, said the cycle passage amounted to a few sentences that "remained completely unintelligible" to anyone not steeped in Böhm-Bawerk.

**Later balanced assessments**
- Moss (1976): the book "cannot be described as either an obscure book or one that has failed to influence the development of monetary economics", yet "Mises' contribution is largely neglected and is no longer considered essential to a mastery of the subject matter of monetary economics"; it has been "for nearly seventy years in limbo between virtual obscurity and academic acclaim"; it "has all the earmarks of a genuine economic classic—it touches on more of the essential problems of monetary economics than any other single work of the first quarter of the twentieth century—but it lacks an acceptable methodological framework for analyzing monetary problems" `[opened]` — [Moss (1976), Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/NPDBooks/Moss/mslLvM.html?chapter_num=4)
- *Concise Encyclopedia of Economics*: the book "was used as a money and banking textbook for the next two decades"; and on tone, "The first section, written in 1912, is calmly argued; the last section, added in the 1940s, is strident." `[opened]` — [CEE, "Ludwig von Mises", Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/bios/Mises.html)
- The English title lost Mises's distinction: *Umlaufsmittel* is "fiduciary media", and the publisher substituted "money and credit" (Wikipedia, citing Hülsmann p. 217 n. 7); Hülsmann calls the English title "smoother but toothless" `[opened]` — [Wikipedia, The Theory of Money and Credit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theory_of_Money_and_Credit); [Hülsmann p. 217 n. 7](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf)
- How small the cycle theory was in 1912: "In 1912 he thought he had found merely one out of a number of conceivably complementary explanations of the business cycle", opening §5 with "It is not the task of this work to develop a theory of economic crises"; from the second edition the qualifications are dropped and the sentence becomes "Our theory of banking . . . leads ultimately to a theory of business cycles." Wikipedia: the theory "appeared in the last few pages" of the book and "emerged as an almost incidental by-product of his exploration of the theory of banking" `[opened]` — [Hülsmann p. 254](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf); [Wikipedia, Austrian business cycle theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory)

### Inferences
- Three of the five named receptions are by partisans or pupils (Robbins in 1934, Hayek, Rothbard). The essay should not cite Robbins's or Rothbard's praise as "the reception"; the independent contemporaries (Keynes, Wicksell) and the independent later readers (Hicks, Patinkin via Moss) were critical. Keynes's later footnote weakens his 1914 verdict but does not reverse it: he never re-reviewed the book.
- Hayek's 1931/35 footnote is the fairest one-line characterisation for the essay: in the 1912/1924 text the cycle mechanism is a few sentences resting on Böhm-Bawerk, and the developed "Austrian theory" is Hayek's (1931) and Mises's own later work (1928, 1940, 1949). Anything the essay attributes to "the book" about cycles should be confined to those few sentences (see sibling file `tmc_credit_interest_cycle.md`).
- The English text is not a complete witness to the German (Robbins: "Certain passages … have been omitted"), and the English title obscures the book's actual subject, fiduciary media.

### Gaps
- Keynes's review was not opened on JSTOR; the wording above is Machlup's transcription with his elisions. I could not confirm from an opened source Keynes's reported judgment that the section on the value of money was the weak part.
- Wicksell's review: neither the review (reprinted, per a Taylor & Francis listing, in a Routledge volume of Wicksell's writings on money) nor Festré's article was opened; no direct quotation from Wicksell is available here.
- Hicks: the 1935 article was not opened; Hicks's "The Hayek Story" (in *Critical Essays in Monetary Theory*, 1967) concerns Hayek's *Prices and Production*, not Mises's book, and was not researched.
- Hayek's later retrospective tributes to Mises (e.g. in *The Fortunes of Liberalism*, Collected Works vol. 4) were not found in opened or snippet form; only his 1933–35 statements are reported.

## 2. What are the strongest criticisms of the Mises–Hayek cycle theory? Friedman, Krugman, Tullock, Caplan, rational expectations, and the empirical tests. What does the evidence support and what does it not?

### Takeaway
At full strength the case is: (i) the data contradict the claim that the size of a boom determines the size of the bust (Friedman); (ii) the theory cannot explain why a past investment mistake should produce mass unemployment rather than a shift of labour, since an investment boom should tighten the labour market (Tullock, Krugman); (iii) it requires entrepreneurs to be fooled, cycle after cycle, by an interest-rate signal they know to be temporary (Tullock, Caplan, the rational-expectations objection; Hayek himself called a theory resting on "a false step by bankers" "inevitably suspect"); (iv) its capital theory was judged defective in the 1930s–40s (Sraffa, Kaldor) and its do-nothing policy conclusion did harm (Friedman). Econometric support is thin and comes mostly from the Austrian journal: monetary shocks do move the term structure and output (Keeler 2001; Bismans & Mougeot 2009), but the distinctive relative-price and stage-of-production effects were not found (Lester & Wolff 2013). What mainstream evidence does support is a weaker cousin: credit booms misallocate labour and precede crises (BIS work), which is not the same as Mises's mechanism. Almost none of this criticism is aimed at the 1912 book; it is aimed at Hayek (1931), Rothbard, and Mises's later statements.

### Cited Findings

**Who each critic is actually criticising**
- Wikipedia's summary: the theory "is generally rejected by mainstream economists" (citing Friedman 1969, Friedman 1993, Krugman 1998, Hummel 1979); "According to John Quiggin, most economists believe that the Austrian business cycle theory is incorrect because of its incompleteness and other problems" `[opened]` — [Wikipedia, Austrian business cycle theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory)
- Sraffa (1932, *Economic Journal* 42, pp. 42–53) and Kaldor (1942, *Economica* 9(36), pp. 359–382) attacked Hayek's version: Sraffa "argued that Hayek's theory did not explain why "forced savings" induced by inflation would generate investments in capital that were inherently less sustainable than those induced by voluntary savings" and that it "failed to define a single "natural" rate of interest"; Kaldor wrote that Hayek's work had "fascinated the academic world of economists" but that attempts to fill the gaps made them appear "larger, instead of smaller" until "one was driven to the conclusion that the basic hypothesis of the theory, that scarcity of capital causes crises, must be wrong" `[opened: Wikipedia's paraphrase and quotation]` — [Wikipedia, Austrian business cycle theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory)
- Tullock's target was Rothbard's popular pamphlet *Economic Depressions: Their Cause and Cure* (1969), as Salerno complains ("the version that is chosen for criticism (Rothbard [1969]) was intended as a popular exposition") `[opened]` — [Salerno, "Comment on Tullock's 'Why Austrians Are Wrong About Depressions'", *Review of Austrian Economics* 3 (1989), pp. 141–145](https://cdn.mises.org/RAE3_1_11.pdf)
- Friedman names Mises directly ("the Mises cycle theory is a clear example"), but means the boom-causes-bust proposition in general, not the 1912 text (see below).

**Friedman: the "plucking model" and the empirical objection**
- Sources: Friedman, "The Monetary Studies of the National Bureau", in *The National Bureau Enters Its 45th Year, 44th Annual Report* (1964), pp. 7–25, reprinted in *The Optimum Quantity of Money and Other Essays* (Aldine, 1969; Wikipedia prints "Optimal"), ch. 12, pp. 261–284; and "The 'Plucking Model' of Business Fluctuations Revisited", *Economic Inquiry* 31(2), April 1993, pp. 171–177 `[opened: citations as given by Wikipedia; Friedman's papers not opened]` — [Wikipedia, Austrian business cycle theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory)
- The finding, in Friedman's words as quoted by Huerta de Soto (1993 paper, p. 172; data for 1961–1987): no correlation between the size of an expansion and the size of the following contraction, which "would cast grave doubt on those theories that see as the source of a deep depression the excesses of the prior expansion (the Mises cycle theory is a clear example)." `[opened: Huerta de Soto's quotation]` — [Huerta de Soto, *Money, Bank Credit, and Economic Cycles* (2nd English ed., Mises Institute), p. 495 n. 103](https://cdn.mises.org/Money_Bank_Credit_and_Economic_Cycles_De%20Soto.pdf)
- The model, as summarised in the Austrian journal itself: "Economies do not substantially go over trend during a boom; they collapse and then return to trend. Recessions "pluck" output downwards, but booms do not have similar effects in the opposite direction … Therefore, busts are what is to be explained, not the boom. While defenders of ABCT have objected to this interpretation (e.g. Garrison 1996), it remains an effective rhetorical point among macroeconomists." `[opened]` — [Ryan H. Murphy, "The Plucking Model, the Great Recession, and Austrian Business Cycle Theory", *Quarterly Journal of Austrian Economics* 18(1), 2015, pp. 40–44](https://cdn.mises.org/QJAE_18_no.%201_Spring%202015%20Murphy%20Plucking%20Model.pdf)
- A sharper sentence widely attributed to Friedman: "The Hayek-Mises explanation of the business cycle is contradicted by the evidence. It is, I believe, false." `[snippet: reproduced in a 1995 FEE article, which ties it to the 1993 paper; not checked in Friedman]` — [FEE, "Economics on Trial" (1995)](https://fee.org/articles/economics-on-trial/)
- Policy objection (interview, *Barron's*, 24 August 1998): "I think the Austrian business-cycle theory has done the world a great deal of harm. If you go back to the 1930s, which is a key point, here you had the Austrians sitting in London, Hayek and Lionel Robbins, and saying you just have to let the bottom drop out of the world. … I think by encouraging that kind of do-nothing policy both in Britain and in the United States, they did harm." `[opened: as quoted by Wikipedia]` — [Wikipedia, Austrian business cycle theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory)
- Austrian replies: Garrison (1996, *Economic Inquiry* 34(4), pp. 799–802): "Friedman's empirical findings are broadly consistent with both Monetarist and Austrian views", because the plucking model works at the highest level of aggregation while the Austrian claim concerns the composition of output `[opened: as quoted by Wikipedia]`. Huerta de Soto: Friedman uses GDP, which "conceal[s] nearly half of the total gross national output"; the theory "establishes a correlation between credit expansion, microeconomic malinvestment and recession, not between economic expansion and recession"; and 1961–1987 is too short `[opened]`. Murphy (2015): after 2008 US real GDP "did not snap back to potential as predicted by the plucking model" `[opened]` — [Wikipedia, ABCT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory); [Huerta de Soto p. 495 n. 103](https://cdn.mises.org/Money_Bank_Credit_and_Economic_Cycles_De%20Soto.pdf); [Murphy (2015)](https://cdn.mises.org/QJAE_18_no.%201_Spring%202015%20Murphy%20Plucking%20Model.pdf)

**Krugman, "The Hangover Theory" (Slate, posted 4 December 1998)**
- Krugman opened by saying he regarded the Austrian theory of the business cycle as about as worthy of serious study as "the phlogiston theory of fire", called the idea the overinvestment theory, "liquidationism" or the "hangover theory", and concluded that it is intellectually incoherent because nobody has explained "why bad investments in the past require the unemployment of good workers in the present" `[snippet: both phrases confirmed only in secondary reproductions; the Slate page was not opened. Wikipedia's reference gives the date 1998-12-04 and the original URL slate.com/id/9593]` — [Krugman, "The Hangover Theory", Slate](https://slate.com/business/1998/12/the-hangover-theory.html); [Wikipedia, ABCT (reference list)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory)
- Austrian reply, as characterised in search results: Krugman misstates the theory as "overinvestment" when it is "malinvestment" (wrong composition, not too much) `[snippet]` — [Garrison, reply to Krugman](https://webhome.auburn.edu/~garriro/krugman.htm)

**Tullock (Review of Austrian Economics 2, pp. 73–78; dated 1988 by Wikipedia, 1987 by some indexes)**
- Tullock's three "nits" and major objection, as reported by his critic Salerno: (1) "Rothbard never explains why the inflation which is part of his theory cannot simply be continued or even accelerated."; (2) why entrepreneurs "do not eventually learn about, correctly forecast, and adjust their investment activities to the business cycle" (Salerno: "In current jargon, the author is questioning why Austrian cycle theorists do not assume that market participants are capable of formulating "rational expectations""); (3) statistical tests suggest booms and depressions follow a "random walk", not a cycle; major objection: during an Austrian cycle "there would be only minor transitional unemployment [and] measured GNP would be higher as a result" (Tullock p. 74) `[opened: Salerno's report; Tullock's original article returned HTTP 403 and was not opened]` — [Salerno (1989), pp. 141–142](https://cdn.mises.org/RAE3_1_11.pdf)
- Tullock in his own words (reply, 1989): "My point was that the mechanism described by the Austrians would lead, not to unemployment, but to a shortage of labor and higher wages than would be justified without all of this additional capital in the market." It might "increase bankruptcies and suicides by brokers, occasionally lead to incomplete factories, and cause a certain amount of transitional unemployment, particularly in the capital-goods industries, but it could not create the kind of massive unemployment we saw in 1929-33, 1937, or the only depression that he mentions, 1980-82." `[opened]` — [Tullock, "Reply to Comment by Joseph T. Salerno", *Review of Austrian Economics* 3 (1989), pp. 147–149](https://cdn.mises.org/rae3_1_12.pdf)
- "The basic flaw in the Austrian's line of reasoning as I see it is the desire to argue that the inflation that they object to is the actual cause of the subsequent contraction. ## 3. What is the case against the gold standard as a monetary constitution? Eichengreen, Bernanke & James, Friedman & Schwartz

### Takeaway
The standard case is that the gold standard bought long-run price stability at the cost of short-run price and output instability and, between the wars, transmitted deflation internationally and stopped central banks from offsetting banking panics; countries recovered from the Depression in the order in which they left gold. Friedman and Schwartz put the US contraction down to Federal Reserve failure rather than to gold as such, a point later pressed by Bordo, Choudhri & Schwartz (2002).

### Cited Findings
- Three-sentence version, each clause sourced. (1) The interwar gold standard is blamed by "Barry Eichengreen, Peter Temin, and Ben Bernanke" on "two arguments: "(1) Under the gold standard, deflationary shocks were transmitted between countries and, (2) for most countries, continued adherence to gold prevented monetary authorities from offsetting banking panics and blocked their recoveries."" (the quotation is from Bordo, Choudhri & Schwartz 2002, summarising the view) `[opened]`. (2) Eichengreen's *Golden Fetters: The Gold Standard and the Great Depression, 1919–1939* (Oxford University Press, 1992) argues the interwar gold standard was a central factor in the worldwide Depression and that recovery became possible only once countries abandoned it, the main evidence being that countries leaving gold earlier recovered sooner `[snippet: NBER book page and reviews]`; "A 2024 study in the American Economic Review found that for a sample of 27 countries, leaving the gold standard helped states to recover from the Great Depression" (Ellison, Lee & O'Rourke, "The Ends of 27 Big Depressions", AER 114(1), pp. 134–168) `[opened: Wikipedia]`. (3) Bernanke & James, "The Gold Standard, Deflation, and Financial Crisis in the Great Depression: An International Comparison" (NBER Working Paper 3488, 1990; in Hubbard (ed.), *Financial Markets and Financial Crises*, 1991) is the international comparison tying gold adherence to deflation and banking crises `[snippet]` — [Wikipedia, Gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard); [NBER, Golden Fetters](https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/golden-fetters-gold-standard-and-great-depression-1919-1939); [NBER WP 3488](https://www.nber.org/papers/w3488)
- Friedman & Schwartz (*A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960*, 1963): the Depression "was due to the Federal Reserve letting the money supply shrink from 1929 to 1933"; among the mistakes, in autumn 1931 the Fed "raised interest rates to defend the dollar in response to speculative attacks, ignoring the difficulties this caused to domestic commercial banks" `[opened: Wikipedia; the "Thesis" section there is flagged as unreferenced]` — [Wikipedia, A Monetary History of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Monetary_History_of_the_United_States)
- The qualification from the monetarist side: the second argument applies "to small open economies with limited gold reserves. This was not the case for the United States … The United States was not constrained from using expansionary policy to offset banking panics, deflation, and declining economic activity." - The numbers, from a sympathetic-to-rules monetary historian (Michael Bordo): US inflation averaged 0.1 per cent a year in 1880–1914 against 4.1 per cent in 1946–2003, but "because economies under the gold standard were so vulnerable to real and monetary shocks, prices were highly unstable in the short run": the coefficient of variation of annual price changes was 17.0 in 1879–1913 against 0.88 in 1946–1990; for real output 3.5 in 1879–1913 against 0.4 in 1946–2003; US unemployment "averaged 6.8 percent … between 1879 and 1913, and 5.9 percent between 1946 and 2003" `[opened]` — [Bordo, "Gold Standard", *Concise Encyclopedia of Economics*, Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/GoldStandard.html)

## 4. What are the interpretive disputes among Mises scholars? Free banking versus 100 per cent reserves; whose "natural rate"; the status of the regression theorem

### Takeaway
Three live disputes. (a) Banking: Mises's texts support both readings, because he wrote in 1912 that "the only way of eliminating human influence on the credit system is to suppress all further issue of fiduciary media", proposed "a rigid 100 percent reserve for all future deposits" for the United States in 1952, and wrote in 1949 that "Free banking is the only method available for the prevention of the dangers inherent in credit expansion"; White and Selgin read him as a fractional-reserve free banker, Salerno and Huerta de Soto as a 100-per-cent-reserve man who saw free banking as the route to it. (b) Natural rate: the 1912 concept is Wicksell's distinction filled with Böhm-Bawerk's capital theory, which Mises disowned in a footnote and replaced in 1940/1949 with time-preference interest and a different benchmark. (c) Regression theorem: Patinkin showed it is not logically required to escape circularity; defenders recast it as a historical or behavioural claim; Bitcoin has reopened it.

The basic conception of Peel's Act ought to be restated and more completely implemented than it was in the England of his time by including the issue of credit in the form of bank balances within the legislative prohibition." `[opened: local primary text]` — [Econlib, TMC ch. 20](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=24)

**(b) Whose natural rate, and how Mises revised it**
- Hülsmann: Mises "could rely on two important discoveries of previous thinkers. The first was the business-cycle theory of the British Currency School … The second was Knut Wicksell's discovery that monetary expansion could result from discrepancies between the money rate of interest and the equilibrium rate of interest." (pp. 251–252); Wicksell "defined the natural rate of interest as the rate that would come into existence under the sole influence of real (non-monetary) factors" and "also defined it as the rate at which the price level would remain constant", and "Both distinctions led to great confusion among later theorists" (p. 253 n. 79) `[opened]` — [Hülsmann pp. 251–253](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf)
- The revision: "In his Theory of Money and Credit, Mises had based his analysis on the Wicksellian distinction between the natural rate of interest and the money rate. But this distinction was untenable in light of Mises's work on economic calculation and on the non-neutrality of money. There is no such thing as a natural rate of interest, defined as the rate of interest that would prevail in a barter economy. … The relevant benchmark was no longer the Wicksellian natural rate that would exist if the economy were a barter economy. It was rather the monetary interest rate that would exist in the absence of credit expansion." (pp. 779–780, on *Nationalökonomie*, 1940) `[opened]` — [Hülsmann pp. 779–780](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf)
- Continuity of the mechanism: the cycle exposition is textually the same in the first and later editions ("the same discussion of forced savings, the reverse movement of prices, the natural rate of interest and deviations from it induced through fiduciary media, the importance of the subsistence fund", p. 254 n. 83) `[opened]` — [Hülsmann p. 254 n. 83](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf)
- Garrison's lineage (quoted by Wikipedia): the theory is "built on the vision of a capital-using production process developed in Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk's Capital and Interest", while "Knut Wicksell's Interest and Prices … provided the basis for the Austrian account of the misallocation of capital during the boom" and the turning-point mechanism "resembles an analogous process described by the British Currency School" `[opened]` — [Wikipedia, Austrian business cycle theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_business_cycle_theory)

**(c) The status of the regression theorem**
- What it claims (Moss): "any object presently used as money is ultimately linked to some commodity that was originally directly serviceable to men's wants … The obvious implication of this theorem is that government, no matter how powerful, cannot introduce an object as money unless it first defines that object in terms either of a money already existing or of a commodity whose market value is already established." `[opened]` — [Moss (1976), Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/NPDBooks/Moss/mslLvM.html?chapter_num=4)
- The standard criticism (Moss, following Patinkin): "In his assertion that the only way the demand for money can be consistently incorporated into the general body of utility theory is by introducing historical prices, Mises is quite mistaken. Patinkin demonstrated how to derive a demand curve for money without resorting to past price behavior"; Mises's statements about the form utility theory must take "are largely incorrect and result from a failure to distinguish adequately between the "utility of money" and the "utility of the services provided by money."" Moss nevertheless credits Mises with "a bold empirical hypothesis of the way individuals behave in a market economy" and notes that in Patinkin's model "the money commodity can be any of the many commodities available", which loses what Mises was trying to explain `[opened]` — [Moss (1976), Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/library/NPDBooks/Moss/mslLvM.html?chapter_num=4)

## 5. Did Mises himself write about social security, pensions, saving for old age or population? Exact locations. Did he do so anywhere in *The Theory of Money and Credit*?

### Takeaway
Yes, but not in TMC. The key texts are *Human Action* (1949): ch. XX §2 (the masses are creditors through, among other things, "social security benefits"), ch. ## 7. Does the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy or another neutral reference characterise Mises's monetary theory, and how?

### Takeaway
The Stanford Encyclopedia has no entry on Ludwig von Mises and its entry on the philosophy of money never names him; it treats the commodity theory of money (the family Mises's regression theorem belongs to) as a classic view with "serious and sustained critique" and mentions gold-standard advocacy only through Rothbard. The neutral-to-sympathetic references that do characterise his monetary theory describe it as the extension of marginal-utility theory to money plus a credit-expansion theory of the cycle (CEE), and as a cycle theory "generally rejected by mainstream economists" (Wikipedia).

### Inferences
- "Neutral reference" is hard to satisfy for Mises: the fullest online treatments (Econlib, the Mises Institute, Liberty Fund forums) are published by sympathetic institutions, and the SEP's silence is itself the signal. For the essay, the accurate framing is "a heterodox classic": influential in German-language economics before 1930, the source of Hayek's cycle theory, and outside the mainstream since the 1930s.
- The SEP passage is useful for one balanced sentence: the worry that states and banks over-issue credit money is a recognised normative criticism, and the commodity-money and gold-standard response to it is a minority position.
