## Quote exactly, with paragraph ids, the passage that places sound money in the same class as political constitutions and bills of rights, and the sentences around it that explain the principle's affirmative and negative sides.

### Takeaway
The passage is IV.21.4 (ch. 21 §1, written 1952, published 1953): sound money "was devised as an instrument for the protection of civil liberties against despotic inroads on the part of governments. Ideologically it belongs in the same class with political constitutions and bills of rights." The two sides are stated in IV.21.6: affirmative in "approving the market's choice of a commonly used medium of exchange", negative in "obstructing the government's propensity to meddle with the currency system". IV.21.13 and IV.23.60 turn the comparison into a claim about budget control: without a metallic check, parliamentary control of spending and "all other constitutional safeguards" can be evaded.

  > [IV.21.3 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §1 The Classical Idea of Sound Money] How keep under control the men entrusted with the handling of the government apparatus lest they turn their weapons against those whom they were expected to serve? The main political problem is how to prevent the rulers from becoming despots and enslaving the citizenry.
- The key passage, quoted in full (the whole of IV.21.4). — [Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25)
  > [IV.21.4 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §1 The Classical Idea of Sound Money] It is impossible to grasp the meaning of the idea of sound money if one does not realize that it was devised as an instrument for the protection of civil liberties against despotic inroads on the part of governments. Ideologically it belongs in the same class with political constitutions and bills of rights. The demand for constitutional guarantees and for bills of rights was a reaction against arbitrary rule and the nonobservance of old customs by kings. The postulate of sound money was first brought up as a response to the princely practice of debasing the coinage.   > [IV.21.5 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §1 The Classical Idea of Sound Money] The censure is spurious as it refers merely to the grammatical form of the idea and does not comprehend that all civil rights can be as well defined in affirmative as in negative terms. ### Inferences
- The word is "Ideologically": Mises classifies an idea by its purpose. He does not say sound money was written into constitutions, and the essay should not imply that he did.
- The comparison is with *negative* constitutional rules (things a government may not do). That fits a rule about money creation. It does not transfer automatically to a pension promise, which is a positive claim on future contributors. Any link to the essay's pay-as-you-go section is an analogy about hidden versus voted costs, not an identity.
- The principle as Mises states it constrains one financing channel only. IV.23.12 says how much a government borrows from the public is "irrelevant to the treatment of monetary problems" (quoted under the public-finance question), so sound money in his sense puts no ceiling on debt or on unfunded promises.

### Gaps
- Page numbers were not verified. The 1953 Yale edition was not seen; Econlib reproduces the 1981 Liberty Fund text. Hülsmann's page citations to a Liberty Fund printing (pp. 468, 493, 494, 500) are not mutually consistent with the paragraph order, so use paragraph ids, not his pages.
- I did not check whether the 1953 Yale wording differs anywhere from the Liberty Fund text hosted by Econlib.
- I did not trace where else Mises makes the constitutional comparison (for example in *Human Action*); that is outside this brief.

## What does Mises say about who the creditors are in a modern economy (savings deposits, life insurance, bonds, pensions, social security) and who the debtors are? Quote any passage where he says the masses are creditors, with ids.

### Takeaway
One paragraph carries the claim, IV.21.17 (ch. 21 §2, written 1952): under capitalism the typical debtors are "the well-to-do owners of real estate, of firms, and of common stock", and the typical creditors are "people of modest means who own bonds and savings accounts or have taken out insurance policies"; of "the common man" he says "he himself is a creditor". The list is bonds, savings accounts and insurance policies. Pensions and social security are not in it, the government is not named among the debtors, and Mises gives no figures.

### Inferences
- The brief's list (c) includes pension and social-security claims. Mises's list does not. Extending his claim that the common man is a creditor to pension claimants is the essay's step, not his, and should be labelled as such.
- His creditors hold *nominal* claims fixed in money. A pay-as-you-go pension is a claim on future contributions that is usually indexed by law, so inflation does not erode it by the mechanism of IV.21.17. For context, United States Social Security benefits had no automatic cost-of-living adjustment when Mises wrote; the adjustment was legislated in 1972 and took effect in 1975. — [Wikipedia, History of Social Security in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Social_Security_in_the_United_States)
- Mises himself ranks the creditor–debtor transfer below the uneven-price effects (IV.23.67–68). An essay that presents him as chiefly a defender of creditors would misstate his own emphasis.
- The government is absent from the "typical debtors" sentence. In Part IV the state gains from inflation as a spender that avoids a vote (next question), not as a debtor whose bonds shrink.

## How does he describe inflation as a method of public finance and its relation to representative government and budget control (taxation without legislation, concealed costs)?

### Takeaway
In the 1952 text inflation is spending that no legislature authorised and no voter knowingly paid for: it lets rulers elude "the financial and budgetary prerogatives of the representative assemblies" [IV.21.13], it works only through "the ignorance of the public" [IV.21.19], and it supplies funds the government "could neither collect by taxation nor borrow from the savings of the public" [IV.21.48]. The phrases "taxation without legislation", "hidden tax" and "inflation tax" do not occur in Part IV or the prefaces. Mises also says, twice, that the main inflationary motive of his day was *not* fiscal but the full-employment policy [IV.23.20; HP.6 for 1934].

- The voters' own inconsistency. — [Econlib ch. 23](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=27)
  > [IV.23.76 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §6 Concluding Remarks] People lament over inflation, but they enthusiastically support policies that could not go on without inflation. **The "emergency" argument (ch. 21 §4)**
- Mises treats this as the only serious argument for inflation, held by people who accept sound-money economics. — [Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25)
  > [IV.21.43 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] In full awareness of all this, however, they still believe that there are emergencies which peremptorily require or at least justify recourse to inflation.
- His answer: real resources must come from consumption, output or capital whatever the financing, so a people that agrees can tax itself. — [Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25)
  > [IV.21.44 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] Whether there is inflation or not, the material equipment required by the armed forces must be provided out of the available means by restricting consumption for nonvital purposes, by intensifying production in order to increase output, and by consuming a part of the capital previously accumulated.
  > [IV.21.44 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] The great emergency can be dealt with without recourse to inflation.
- The real "emergency" is a majority that will not pay; inflation then is a way round the electorate. — [Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25)
  > [IV.21.45 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] They reject the democratic way of persuading the majority. They arrogate to themselves the power and the moral right to circumvent the will of the people. They are eager to win its cooperation by deceiving the public about the costs involved in the measures suggested. While seemingly complying with the constitutional procedures of representative government, their conduct is in effect not that of elected officeholders but that of guardians of the people.
  > [IV.21.46 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] The emergency that brings about inflation is this: the people or the majority of the people are not prepared to defray the costs incurred by their rulers' policies. They support these policies only to the extent that they believe their conduct does not burden themselves. They vote, for instance, only for such taxes as are to be paid by other people, namely, the rich, because they think that these taxes do not impair their own material well-being.
  > [IV.21.46 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] But if the government resorts for this purpose to inflation, it is employing methods which are contrary to the principles of representative government, although formally it may have fully complied with the letter of the constitution.   > [IV.21.47 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] It is not just an accident that in our age inflation has become the accepted method of monetary management.   > [IV.21.48 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] Recourse to inflation may provide the government with the funds which it could neither collect by taxation nor borrow from the savings of the public because the people and its parliamentary representatives objected.

**The full-employment doctrine (ch. 21 §3)**

  > [IV.21.35 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §3 The Full-Employment Doctrine] There is only one efficacious way toward a rise in real wage rates and an improvement of the standard of living of the wage earners: to increase the per-head quota of capital invested.

Credit expansion is subservient to the unions.
  > [HP.6 | Preface to the English Edition, signed Vienna, June 1934] But the motive for recent experiments in depreciation has been by no means fiscal. ### Inferences
- "Taxation without legislation" is not Mises's phrase here. If the essay wants that idea in his words, the exact supports are IV.21.13 ("unauthorized expenditures"), IV.23.12 ("by virtue of duly promulgated laws") and IV.21.46 ("cheating the voters instead of trying to convince them").
- Possible bridge to the essay's section 10, to be labelled analogy: Mises objects to a financing method whose cost falls on people who never voted it and cannot see it. In the "benefits held" pole of a pay-as-you-go scheme the rising contribution rate is legislated and visible, but it falls on cohorts who were not party to the original promise. The shared feature is cost shifted away from the deciding electorate. The mechanisms differ (money creation versus intergenerational contribution rates), and Mises does not discuss the second.
- IV.21.46 (people vote "only for such taxes as are to be paid by other people, namely, the rich") is the nearest thing in Part IV to a political-economy statement that could be set beside pension politics. It concerns the rich as the "other people", not the young or the unborn.
- Mises's own ranking of motives (employment first, treasury second) limits how far Part IV can be used as a text about fiscal dominance.

### Gaps
- Mises does not identify the "professors" beyond footnote 4, and I did not read Lerner pp. 307–8 or Ruml's article to check his paraphrase of them.
- Econlib's footnote reads "A. B. Lerner"; I could not check the 1953 or 1981 print to see whether the misprint is Econlib's or the edition's.

## What does chapter 22 say about the gold-exchange standard, the 'flexible standard' and holding foreign exchange as reserves? What does chapter 23 propose for the United States and for other countries, and what does it say about the dollar's international position, the IMF and foreign aid?

### Takeaway
Chapter 22 (written 1952) classifies four regimes: the pre-1914 gold and gold-exchange standards with a legal parity; the interwar "flexible standard", where an agency sets and changes the parity; the "freely vacillating currency", of which the dollar is the "outstanding instance"; and the "illusive standard" of exchange control. Chapter 23 gives a poor country ("Ruritania") a conversion agency (in today's terms something like a currency board; that label is mine) pegged to "gold or the dollar" with 100 percent marginal cover, and gives the United States a full gold-coin standard at a new market-found parity, probably above $35. The IMF gets one dismissive clause. Foreign aid, the Marshall Plan, Bretton Woods and "reserve currency" are not mentioned at all.

### Cited Findings
**Gold-exchange standard before 1914 (ch. 22 §1)**
- A legal, unchangeable parity is the mark of both standards; the gold-exchange standard differed only in degree, and its central bank sold "gold bullion and foreign exchange". — [Econlib ch. 22](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=26)
  > [IV.22.1 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §1 The Inflexible Gold Standard] The mark of all the varieties of the gold standard and the gold-exchange standard as they existed on the eve of World War I was the gold parity of the country's monetary unit, precisely determined by a duly promulgated law. It was understood that this parity would never be changed.
  > [IV.22.2 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §1 The Inflexible Gold Standard] The difference between the standard that was later called the orthodox or the classical gold standard and the gold-exchange standard was a difference of degree.
  > [IV.22.2 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §1 The Inflexible Gold Standard] Under the gold-exchange standard no gold was used in transacting domestic business. But the central bank sold gold bullion and foreign exchange against domestic currency at rates that did not exceed the legal parity by more than the gold margin would be under the classical gold standard. Thus the countries under the gold-exchange standard were no less integrated into the system of the international gold standard than those under the classical gold standard.

**The flexible standard (ch. 22 §2)**
- Origin, then four numbered features. The numbered items are on the Econlib page but missing from the local text file, and have no paragraph ids. On the page the order is: IV.22.3, items 1–3, IV.22.4 (which continues item 3), item 4. — [Econlib ch. 22](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=26)
  > [IV.22.3 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §2 The Flexible Standard] The flexible standard, a development of the period between World War I and World War II, originated from the gold-exchange standard.
  > [numbered item 1 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §2 The Flexible Standard | Econlib page only, no paragraph id] The domestic standard's parity as against gold and foreign exchange is not fixed by a law but simply by the government agency entrusted with the conduct of monetary affairs.
  > [numbered item 2 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §2 The Flexible Standard | Econlib page only, no paragraph id] This parity is subject to sudden changes without previous notice to the public. It is flexible. But this flexibility is practically always employed for lowering the domestic currency's exchange value as against gold and those foreign currencies which did not drop against gold.
  > [numbered item 3 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §2 The Flexible Standard | Econlib page only, no paragraph id] The only method available for preventing a currency's exchange value from dropping below the parity chosen is unconditional redemption of any amount offered.
  > [IV.22.4 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §2 The Flexible Standard] Of course, this point--the parity--is not fixed by a law under the flexible standard, and the agency is free to decline to buy an amount offered at this rate.
  > [IV.22.4 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §2 The Flexible Standard] Such an event is sometimes referred to as raising the price of gold.
  > [numbered item 4 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §2 The Flexible Standard | Econlib page only, no paragraph id] In some countries the conduct of pegging operations is entrusted to the central bank, in others to a special agency called foreign-exchange equalization account or a similar name.

There is no redemption either de jure or de facto. The pieces are not money substitutes but money proper in themselves.
  > [IV.22.5 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §3 The Freely Vacillating Currency] With regard to these effects the freely vacillating currency may be called a bad currency. But it can last and is not inevitably headed for a breakdown.

  > [IV.22.8 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §3 The Freely Vacillating Currency] The outstanding instance of a freely vacillating currency today is the United States dollar, the New Deal dollar. It is not redeemable in gold or any foreign exchange. The administration is committed to an inflationary policy, increasing more and more the amount of notes in circulation and of bank deposits subject to check. If the Treasury had been permitted to act according to the designs of its advisers, the dollar would have long since gone the way of the German mark of 1923.   > [IV.23.65 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §5 The Controversy Concerning the Choice of the New Gold Parity] To hold gold was a criminal offense for the residents of the United States.

**Illusive standard, exchange control and the "scarcity of dollars" (ch. 22 §4)**
- The official parity is a fiction enforced by confiscating foreign exchange; the resulting "shortage" is a price-control effect. — [Econlib ch. 22](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=26)
  > [IV.22.9 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §4 The Illusive Standard] The illusive standard is based on a falsehood. The government decrees that there exists a parity between the domestic currency and gold or foreign exchange.
  > [IV.22.12 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §4 The Illusive Standard] The illusive standard and its main tool, foreign-exchange control, result in a state of affairs which is--rather inappropriately--called shortage of foreign exchange.
  > [IV.22.13 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §4 The Illusive Standard] To establish the fact that gold or dollars are in short supply is to pronounce a truism.
  > [IV.22.14 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §4 The Illusive Standard] The state of affairs which those talking of a scarcity of dollars want to describe is this: At the fictitious parity, arbitrarily fixed by the government and enforced by the whole governmental apparatus of oppression and compulsion, demand for dollars exceeds the supply of dollars offered for sale.
  > [IV.22.15 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §4 The Illusive Standard] The Ruritanians would like to consume more foreign goods than they can buy by exporting Ruritanian products.
  > [IV.22.15 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §4 The Illusive Standard] Their plight is brought about by the fact that they are not producing more and better things either for domestic or for foreign consumption.
- Consequence for international capital. — [Econlib ch. 22](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=26)
  > [IV.22.17 | ch. 22 Contemporary Currency Systems, §4 The Illusive Standard] Foreign-exchange control is today primarily a device for the virtual expropriation of foreign investments. It has destroyed the international capital and money market. It is the main instrument of policies aiming at the elimination of imports and thereby at the economic isolation of the various countries.
- "Dollar gap" was the contemporary name for the postwar shortage Mises is redescribing. — [Wikipedia, Dollar gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_gap)

**Other countries: "Ruritania" (ch. 23 §3)**
- The exchange rate against the dollar depends on inflating no faster than the United States. — [Econlib ch. 23](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=27)
  > [IV.23.25 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §3 Currency Reform in Ruritania] Although today's dollar is no longer the equivalent of the weight of gold it represented under the gold standard, that is, before 1933, 100 paper rurs are needed to buy one of these depreciated dollars.
  > [IV.23.26 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §3 Currency Reform in Ruritania] In fact, in order to maintain a stable exchange rate against the dollar, it would not be forced to abandon inflation altogether.   > [IV.23.28 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §3 Currency Reform in Ruritania] The exchange rate that prevails on the market at this juncture is to be promulgated as the new legal parity between the rur and either gold or the dollar.   > [IV.23.29 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §3 Currency Reform in Ruritania] The reform thus consists of two measures. The first is to end inflation by setting an insurmountable barrier to any further increase in the supply of domestic money.   > [IV.23.45 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §4 The United States' Return to a Sound Currency] At the same time all restrictions on trading and holding gold must be repealed. The free market for gold is to be reestablished.
  > [IV.23.52 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §4 The United States' Return to a Sound Currency] It is essential for the reform suggested that the Federal Reserve System should be kept out of its way.   > [IV.23.50 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §4 The United States' Return to a Sound Currency] A new agency is to be established, the Conversion Agency. - Mises's objection to the gold-exchange standard in 1952 is about domestic visibility (no coins, so no alarm). ## What does he say about the gold standard and war or about governments abandoning gold in order to inflate?

### Takeaway
The 1952 thesis is one sentence: "the gold standard did not collapse. Governments abolished it in order to pave the way for inflation" [IV.21.24]. War enters three ways: the episodes named in IV.21.4 are ones usually classed as war finance, though Mises does not label them so (see Inferences); the emergency argument is at bottom a war-finance argument that he rejects (IV.21.43–48, IV.23.12); and postwar returns to gold at the old parity are his example of how to discredit sound money (IV.23.72–74). The 1924 preface says of 1912–14 that the "most important economic element in this war ideology was inflationism" [HP.17].

### Inferences
- Mises does not say that war destroyed the gold standard. His claim is about intent: governments removed it to be free to inflate, in war and in peace. The essay should keep that distinction.
- The three historical episodes in IV.21.4 (the American continental currency, the paper money of the French Revolution, the British restriction period) are, on my background knowledge and with no source consulted here, cases of wartime or revolutionary finance. Mises does not label them so in the passage; saying so is the essay's gloss and needs its own source.
- The 1952 preface says all countries are "threatened by the gloomy prospect of a complete breakdown of their currencies" [P.1]. If the essay grades forecasts, as its section 11 does for Zeihan, this warning overstated severity, and it should be set beside IV.22.5, where he says a managed fiat currency "can last".

### Gaps

- I did not compare this with what Parts I–III say about war finance.

## Where does Mises qualify these claims himself (for example, on whether a return to gold is politically feasible, or on what the gold standard cannot do)?

### Takeaway
He qualifies more than the polemical tone suggests. In 1952 he rates the political chances "still slim", makes reform conditional on a change of ideology, admits that gold lost purchasing power over the previous century, that fiat currencies "can last", that inflation did cut unemployment in the 1930s, and that the creditor loss is not the main harm and cannot be repaired. In 1934 he wrote that "Gold is not an ideal basis for a monetary system". In 1924 he left the choice among solutions to "politics", not economics.

- A fiat currency "can last and is not inevitably headed for a breakdown" [IV.22.5] if the government shows restraint and the public expects inflation to stop. — [Econlib ch. 22](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=26)
- Inflation did reduce unemployment in the 1930s. — [Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25)
  > [IV.21.40 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §3 The Full-Employment Doctrine] It is a fact that in some countries in the 1930s, recourse to inflation was not immediately followed by a rise in the height of money wage rates as fixed by the governments or unions, that this was tantamount to a drop in real wage rates, and that consequently the number of unemployed decreased. But this was merely a passing phenomenon.
- On the emergency case he grants the government may be right on substance and sincere in motive. — [Econlib ch. 21](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=25)
  > [IV.21.45 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] There is no need to raise the question whether the government's or the majority's opinion is right. Perhaps the government is right.
  > [IV.21.46 | ch. 21 The Principle of Sound Money, §4 The Emergency Argument in Favor of Inflation] The reaction of the government to this attitude of the nation is, at least sometimes, directed by the sincere wish to serve what it believes to be the true interests of the people in the best possible way.
- If prices all moved together, inflation would matter only for deferred payments. — [Econlib ch. 23](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=27)
  > [IV.23.68 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §5 The Controversy Concerning the Choice of the New Gold Parity] If an increase in the quantity of money in circulation were to produce at one blow proportionally the same rise in the prices of every kind of commodities and services, changes in the monetary unit's purchasing power would, apart from affecting deferred payments, be of no social consequence; they would neither benefit nor hurt anybody and would not arouse political unrest.

**Limits on undoing the damage**
- Past losses to creditors cannot be repaired by deflation (IV.23.69, quoted under the creditors question), and even a small deflation is to be avoided. — [Econlib ch. 23](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=27)
  > [IV.23.75 | ch. 23 The Return to Sound Money, §5 The Controversy Concerning the Choice of the New Gold Parity] It is certainly true that a smaller deflation has less undesirable effects than a bigger one.
- On Britain's 1925 return he allowed in 1934 that there were reasons on both sides. — [Econlib historical prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4)
  > [HP.2 | Preface to the English Edition, signed Vienna, June 1934] Of course, there were some reasons for attempting to reestablish the old parity, even despite the indubitable drawbacks of such a proceeding.
- For a small country he settles for a gold-exchange or dollar-exchange standard ("This is enough for the beginning." [IV.23.34]) although he calls the gold-exchange standard incurably defective for the United States (IV.23.58). — [Econlib ch. 23](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=27)

### Inferences
- The fairest one-line summary for the essay: Mises in 1952 presents gold as a political device for keeping money out of the budget process, not as a guarantee of stable prices or of sound public finances, and he does not expect it to be adopted without a change of political philosophy.
- His admission that a restrained fiat regime "can last" (IV.22.5) is the passage to set against the preface's "complete breakdown" (P.1) when the essay states counter-evidence.
- Because IV.23.12 leaves public borrowing outside the monetary question, a writer cannot use Part IV to claim that a gold standard would have prevented unfunded pension promises. It would have removed one way of meeting them.
- Hülsmann's reading supports treating the gold-coin requirement as a change of mind: he calls it "a departure from his thinking in 1934, and even more so from his views of 1912". — [Hülsmann, p. 921](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_2.pdf)

### Gaps
- I did not test any of Mises's empirical concessions or claims (century-long price trend under gold, 1930s unemployment) against data.
- Later assessments of Part IV by monetary historians were not searched; the only secondary source read is Hülsmann, who is sympathetic to Mises.
- The local Parts I–III files were not read for earlier, possibly different, qualifications (for example the 1912 and 1924 treatment of the gold-exchange standard in ch. 20); that comparison rests on Hülsmann alone here.
