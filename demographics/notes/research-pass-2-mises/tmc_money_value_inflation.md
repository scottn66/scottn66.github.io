## Where exactly does Mises argue that money is neither a consumption good nor a production good, and what does he conclude about the quantity of money and social welfare? Give the exact sentences with paragraph ids.

### Takeaway
The argument is ch. 5 §1 (I.5.1–I.5.20): money is a third class of good, a medium of exchange, and because its value adjusts to its quantity, more or less of it leaves society neither richer nor poorer. Two cautions for the essay: Mises says money is **not** a claim (claims are what he calls money substitutes), and he admits two exceptions to "more money adds nothing".

### Cited Findings

**The classification (ch. 5 §1)**

- Opening statement of the thesis — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.1 | ch.5 §1] It is unnecessary to demonstrate that money is not a consumption good. It seems equally incorrect to call it a production good.
- Money is not needed for production as such — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.11 | ch.5 §1] Production is quite possible without money. There is no need for money either in the isolated household or in the socialized community.
- The welfare conclusion, the single most quotable sentence — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.18 | ch.5 §1] An increase in the quantity of money can no more increase the welfare of the members of a community, than a diminution of it can decrease their welfare.
- The mechanism he gives for it, in the same paragraph — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.18 | ch.5 §1] the changes in the value of money are accommodated in such a way to the demand for it that, despite increases or decreases in its quantity, the economic position of mankind remains the same.
- The Adam Smith tag (the ellipsis is in the text; Econlib footnote 54 attaches here) — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.18 | ch.5 §1] Regarded from this point of view, those goods that are employed as money are indeed what Adam Smith called them, "dead stock, which … produces nothing."
- He keeps money "indispensable" while denying it a share in output — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.19 | ch.5 §1] No part of the total result of production is dependent on the collaboration of money, even though the use of money may be one of the fundamental principles on which the economic order is based.
- Restated as the last paragraph of §1 (the local file wrongly places it under §2) — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.20 | ch.5 §1] Production goods derive their value from that of their products. Not so money; for no increase in the welfare of the members of a society can result from the availability of an additional quantity of money.
- Consequence for capital and interest (ch. 5 §§2–3): idle money is not capital, and what matters for interest is goods, not money — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.29 | ch.5 §2] Money that is lying "idle," that is, money that is not exchanged for other goods, is not a part of capital; it produces no fruit.
  > [I.5.31 | ch.5 §3] it is not the greater or smaller quantity of money that is of importance, but the greater or smaller quantity of other economic goods.

**Money versus claims (ch. 3 §1, ch. 5 §1, ch. 8 §6, ch. 13 §4)**

- Mises's definition of a claim, and the three things its value depends on: the goods, the probability of getting them, and time — [ch.3](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=7)
  > [I.3.9 | ch.3 §1] Claims are not goods; they are means of obtaining disposal over goods. This determines their whole nature and economic significance. They themselves are not valued directly, but indirectly; their value is derived from that of the economic goods to which they refer.
  > [I.3.9 | ch.3 §1] Two elements are involved in the valuation of a claim: first, the value of the goods to whose possession it gives a right; and, second, the greater or less probability that possession of the goods in question will actually be obtained.
  > [I.3.9 | ch.3 §1] Furthermore, if the claim is to come into force only after a period of time, then consideration of this circumstance will constitute a third factor in its valuation.
- "Money substitutes" are his name for perfectly secure, immediately convertible claims to money — [ch.3](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=7)
  > [I.3.8 | ch.3 §1] It is considerations such as these that have led the present writer to give the name of money substitutes and not that of money to those objects that are employed like money in commerce but consist in perfectly secure and immediately convertible claims to money.
- The bread-ticket contrast. He reports it as an argument that "may further be pointed out" for counting substitutes as money, grants its weight (I.3.13), and still keeps the narrower definition — [ch.3](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=7)
  > [I.3.12 | ch.3 §1] It may further be pointed out that whereas it is impossible to satisfy an increase in the demand, say, for bread by issuing more breadtickets without adding to the actual supply of bread itself, it is perfectly possible to satisfy an increased demand for money by just such a process as this.
- Money itself is **not** a claim or certificate. This is said against Böhm-Bawerk's "undifferentiated certificates" in a socialist state — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.17 | ch.5 §1] Money is always an economic good, and to say of a claim, which is what a certificate is, that it is like money, is only to drop back into the old practice of regarding rights and business connections as goods.
- Same point for inconvertible paper, where it matters most: credit money began as a claim but is valued as money — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.43 | ch.13 §4] Yet credit money is not merely an acknowledgment of indebtedness and a promise to pay. As money, it has a different standing in the transactions of the market.
- His empirical proof that a note's value is not its value as a claim: in 1884 Austrian 5% government bonds stood at 95.81, below the non-interest-bearing notes of the same debtor (II.8.40); the general rule he states — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.41 | ch.8 §6] Usually a claim to a fixed sum, the date of payment of which lies in the uncertain future, is valued considerably less highly than the sum to which it refers.

**More money and social wealth, restated elsewhere in chs 8, 10, 12, 13, 14 and the prefaces**

- More money means more money income for some, not more goods for all — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.69 | ch.8 §8] An increase in the stock of money in a community always means an increase in the money incomes of a number of individuals; but it need not necessarily mean at the same time an increase in the quantity of goods that are at the disposal of the community, that is to say, it need not mean an increase in the national dividend.
- Any quantity of money does the job — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.78 | ch.8 §8] Society is always in enjoyment of the maximum utility obtainable from the use of money.
- The inflationary boom is a transfer, not a gain — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.32 | ch.12 §3] This is a mistaken view, for an increase in the quantity of money results in no increase of the stock of consumption goods at people's disposal.
  > [II.12.32 | ch.12 §3] for it can only benefit a part of the community at the cost of a corresponding loss by the other part.
  > [II.12.40 | ch.12 §4] The social stock of goods has in no way been increased; the total quantity that can be distributed has remained the same.
- Contrast with a real increase in supply (coal): there, unlike money, society gains — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.31 | ch.12 §3] Many have gained what none have lost. The case of money is different.
- "Naive inflationism" defined as the belief that money is wealth — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.9 | ch.13 §3] It wants more money because in its eyes the mere abundance of money is wealth.
- A currency is not a share in national wealth — [ch.14](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=18)
  > [II.14.9 | ch.14 §2] Such observers fail to recognize that the valuation of the monetary unit depends not upon the wealth of the country, but upon the ratio between the quantity of money and the demand for it, so that even the richest country may have a bad currency and the poorest country a good one.
- Goods are what is finally wanted and what finally pays, between people and between countries — [ch.10](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=14)
  > [II.10.9 | ch.10 §2] Just as the single individual does, so also all the individuals in an economic community taken together, wish in the last analysis to acquire not money, but other economic goods.
  > [II.10.11 | ch.10 §2] It is then immediately clear that goods and services can only be paid for with other goods and services; that in the last analysis there can be no question of payment in money.
- Later prefaces repeat the point (1934 and 1952 text, not 1912/1924) — [1934/1924 prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4); [1952 preface](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=2)
  > [HP.15 | 1934 preface] Money is nothing but a medium of exchange and it completely fulfills its function when the exchange of goods and services is carried on more easily with its help than would be possible by means of barter.
  > [P.2 | 1952 preface] They are the offshoots of doctrines that ascribe to governments the magic power of creating wealth out of nothing and of making people happy by raising the "national income."

**Mises's own qualifications to "more money adds nothing"**

- Exception 1, resource saving: paper that replaces commodity money frees the goods that mining gold would have cost. II.12.32 calls this "the incidental circumstance referred to on page 161 above"; in the 1953 printing the same cross-reference reads "page 138", and that page carries this sentence — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12); cross-reference checked in the [1953 Yale printing](https://cdn.mises.org/The%20Theory%20of%20Money%20and%20Credit_3.pdf)
  > [II.8.69 | ch.8 §8] An increase in the amount of fiat or credit money is only to be regarded as an increase in the stock of goods at the disposal of society if it permits the satisfaction of a demand for money which would otherwise have been satisfied by commodity money instead, since the material for the commodity money would then have had to be procured by the surrender of other goods in exchange or produced at the cost of renouncing some other sort of production.
- Exception 2, indirect effect through who ends up saving — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16); [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.12.32 | ch.12 §3] It is true that this result may be brought about indirectly, in the way in which any change in distribution may affect production as well; that is, by those classes in whose favor the redistribution occurs using their additional command of money to accumulate more capital than would have been accumulated by those people from whom the money was withdrawn.
  > [II.13.12 | ch.13 §3] If the particular conditions of a given case of depreciation are such that wealth is transferred to the rich from the poor, then admittedly saving (and consequently capital accumulation) will be encouraged, production will consequently be stimulated, and so the welfare of posterity increased. In earlier epochs of economic history a moderate inflation may sometimes have had this effect.
- He treats the classification itself as partly a matter of convenience — [ch.5](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=9)
  > [I.5.23 | ch.5 §2] Various opinions may be held as to whether money should be included in the concept of capital or not. The delimitation of concepts of this nature is merely a question of expediency, in connection with which it is quite easy for differences of opinion to arise.
- Secondary check: Hülsmann reads the passage the same way ("inflation and deflation of the money supply could not possibly enhance the productive potential of the nation as a whole", p. 240; "They cannot enrich society as a whole, but do affect distribution, allocation, and incomes within society", p. 243) — [Hülsmann, pp. 240–243](https://cdn.mises.org/Mises%20The%20Last%20Knight%20of%20Liberalism_0.pdf)
- Dating: the welfare sentence of I.5.18 is 1912 text. Hülsmann's collation quotes it and cites it to p. 78 of the first edition ("A78, C101f. See also A263, A402f."), commenting that Mises "emphasizes right from the outset, and then repeatedly throughout the remainder of the book, that there is no relationship between the money supply and aggregate output", and that "Only under exceptional circumstances could an increase of the nominal money supply, directly or indirectly, bring about advantages from the overall point of view" — [Hülsmann 2012, p. 12](https://cdn.mises.org/Theory%20of%20Money%20and%20Fiduciary%20Media.pdf)

### Inferences
- The phrase "money as a claim versus real goods" should not be attributed to Mises as it stands. In his vocabulary money is an economic good that is neither consumed nor used in production; *claims* are a separate category (I.3.9), and he explicitly denies that money is a certificate or claim (I.5.17, II.13.43). What the essay can say exactly is: for Mises, more money adds no goods (I.5.18), and a claim is only "a means of obtaining disposal over goods" (I.3.9).
- A pension entitlement fits Mises's definition of a claim, and his three valuation factors (value of the goods, probability of delivery, time) map onto a pay-as-you-go promise. This is an analogy drawn by the essay, not by Mises: he never applies I.3.9 to pensions, and the word "pension" does not occur in the book.
- The bread-ticket sentence (I.3.12) is tempting for a pay-as-you-go section, but in context Mises uses it to mark what is *special* about money (extra claims can satisfy extra demand for money), not to make a point about real resources. Quote it only with that context.
- I.5.18 is a statement about the total; the whole of ch. 12 is about why the total staying the same still leaves winners and losers. Using I.5.18 without II.12.30 misstates him as a neutrality theorist.

### Gaps
- Only I.5.18 is tied to a 1912 page (A78) by Hülsmann's collation. The other ch. 5 and ch. 3 sentences quoted here (I.5.1, I.5.11, I.5.17, I.5.19, I.5.20, I.3.9, I.3.12) were not individually checked against the 1912 German, which was not readable in this session.

## How does chapter 12 (The Social Consequences of Variations in the Objective Exchange Value of Money) describe the effects on creditors and debtors and on contracts for deferred payment? Which groups does he name?

### Takeaway
Ch. 12 gives two separate channels: unforeseen changes in the value of money rewrite every money contract between creditor and debtor (§1), and, independently of any contract, new money spreads step by step so that early receivers gain from late ones (§3). The groups he names are concrete (mine owners, luxury producers, farmers, war contractors, wage labourers, civil servants, exporters, countries) but he says who wins is an empirical matter case by case, and he never names pensioners, annuitants, widows or orphans.

### Cited Findings

**The frame**

- First sentence of the chapter states both channels — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.1 | ch.12 §1] Variations in the objective exchange value of money evoke displacements in the distribution of income and property, on the one hand because individuals are apt to overlook the variability of the value of money, and on the other hand because variations in the value of money do not affect all economic goods and services uniformly and simultaneously.

**Channel 1: creditors, debtors and deferred payment (ch. 12 §1)**

- People hedge commodity contracts but treat money contracts as riskless — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.2 | ch.12 §1] Lenders and borrowers are not in the habit of allowing for possible future fluctuations in the objective exchange value of money.
  > [II.12.4 | ch.12 §1] In making long-term contracts involving money, the contracting parties are generally unconscious that they are taking part in a speculative transaction.
- The law shares the illusion — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.6 | ch.12 §1] For the law, the invariability of the value of money is not a fiction, but a fact.
- The creditor/debtor rule, with his own bracketed limit — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.11 | ch.12 §1] An increase in the purchasing power of money is disadvantageous to the debtor and advantageous to the creditor; a decrease in its purchasing power has the contrary significance.
  > [II.12.11 | ch.12 §1] (But it is true that neither the extent nor the direction of these variations can be foreseen.)
- Foreseen changes are priced into interest and then redistribute nothing — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.13 | ch.12 §1] Consequently it is not difficult to understand that, so long as continued depreciation is to be reckoned with, those who lend money demand higher rates of interest and those who borrow money are willing to pay the higher rates.
  > [II.12.14 | ch.12 §1] Thus if the direction and extent of variations in the exchange value of money could be foreseen, they would not be able to affect the relations between debtor and creditor; the coming alterations in purchasing power could be sufficiently allowed for in the original terms of the credit transaction.
- But foresight is poor in practice: the same paragraph says the assumption "never holds good except in a most imperfect manner" and that, apart from a few economists, "The value of gold currencies is still regarded as stable" — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.14 | ch.12 §1] The value of gold currencies is still regarded as stable.
- Indexation ("tabular standard": repay the original purchasing power, measured by a bundle of goods) is described and rejected as a cure — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.15 | ch.12 §1] but in credit transactions the outstanding obligation would be discharged, not by payment of the nominal sum of money specified in the contract, but by payment of a sum of money with the purchasing power that the original sum had at the time when the contract was made.
  > [II.12.16 | ch.12 §1] If what is called a weighted average is used, in which individual commodities have an effect proportioned to their significance, then the same consequences will still follow as soon as the conditions of production and consumption alter.
- Legal tender for depreciated notes is part of the state's revenue system, and victims cannot be made whole by special laws — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.17 | ch.12 §1] is a part of the whole system of legal rules which allow the state to exploit its power to create new money as a source of income.
  > [II.12.18 | ch.12 §1] Nevertheless, this evil cannot be counteracted by ad hoc laws designed to remove the burden of the depreciation from single persons, or groups of persons, or classes of the community, and consequently to impose it all the more heavily on others.
  > [II.12.18 | ch.12 §1] If we do not desire the pernicious consequences of depreciation, then we must make up our minds to oppose the inflationary policy by which the depreciation is created.
- The legal version of the same point in ch. 4 — [ch.4](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=8)
  > [I.4.9 | ch.4 §2] When notes that are appraised commercially at only half their face value are proclaimed legal tender, this amounts fundamentally to the same thing as granting debtors legal relief from half of their liabilities.
- Markets answer with gold clauses in long contracts — [ch.4](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=8)
  > [I.4.10 | ch.4 §2] When, during the last decade of the nineteenth century, the bimetallist party in Germany gained so much power that the possibility of experiment with its inflationist proposals had to be reckoned with, gold clauses began to make their appearance in long-term contracts.
- His warning in ch. 1 against seeing *only* the debt channel — [ch.1](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=5)
  > [I.1.18 | ch.1 §3] But it should be pointed out that its use has led many writers to deal with the problems connected with the general economic consequences of changes in the value of money merely from the point of view of modifications in existing debt relations and to overlook their significance in all other connections.

**Channel 1b: false accounts and capital consumption (ch. 12 §2)**

- Depreciation makes capital look like profit, which is then eaten, passed on or taxed — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.24 | ch.12 §2] What is thus improperly regarded as profit, instead of as part of capital, is consumed by the entrepreneur or passed on either to the consumer in the form of price reductions that would not otherwise have been made or to the laborer in the form of higher wages, and the government proceeds to tax it as income or profits.
- Debtors' gains only partly offset it — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.24 | ch.12 §2] But this can never more than partly balance the destruction of capital induced by the depreciation.

**Channel 2: early and late receivers, with the groups he names (ch. 12 §3)**

  > [II.12.33 | ch.12 §3] Finally for the fourth group, the whole process will result in nothing but loss. The farmers will have to pay dearer for all industrial products before they are compensated by the increased prices of agricultural products.
  > [II.12.33 | ch.12 §3] It is these losses of the groups that are the last to be reached by the variation in the value of money which ultimately constitute the source of the profits made by the mine owners and the groups most closely connected with them.

  > [II.12.34 | ch.12 §3] If, for example, there is an issue of paper money in time of war, the new notes will first go into the pockets of the war contractors.
- The general rule and its limit — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.34 | ch.12 §3] The sooner anybody is in a position to adjust his money income to its new value, the more favorable will the process be for him. Which persons, groups, and classes fare better in this, and which worse, depends upon the actual data of each individual case, without knowledge of which we are not in a position to form a judgment.
- Countries: gold producers and their trading partners gain; the last countries reached pay — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)

- Wage labourers and civil servants as late adjusters; the only passage in chs 1–14 about people on slow-moving money incomes — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)

  > [II.12.36 | ch.12 §3] Their multitudinous complaints were partly based on the fact that, since their money incomes could not easily be increased, they had largely to bear the cost of the continual rise in prices. But recently this state of affairs has been changed through the organization of the civil servants on trade-union lines, which has enabled them to secure a quicker response to demands for increases of salaries.
- Rising money is the mirror image: first to accept lower prices lose, last gain — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)

  > [II.12.43 | ch.12 §4] Thus the result is always that the gains of foreign buyers, which in certain cases are shared with home exporters, are counterbalanced by losses that are borne entirely at home.

### Inferences
- Complete list of groups Mises names as gainers or losers in chs 12–14: creditors and debtors; lenders and borrowers; mine owners; producers of luxury goods; "the remaining producers"; agriculturalists/farmers; war contractors; entrepreneurs; wage labourers; civil servants; exporters, importers, foreign buyers and consumers; whole countries (Europe versus the gold-field countries); "all those who are entitled to receive fixed sums of money"; bondholders; labourers, peasants, small industrialists and civil servants with savings deposits or bonds; the state and "its creditors"; the German classes who kept marks and "their families".
- Nobody in chs 1–14 is identified by age or life stage. The nearest thing to a pensioner is the class "entitled to receive fixed sums of money" (II.13.39), and that sentence is about deflation's winners; the inference that the same class loses under inflation is Mises's logic (II.12.11) but not his wording there.
- A pay-as-you-go pension is a deferred-payment contract in Mises's sense only by analogy. The closest structural parallel is II.12.33: the group reached last bears the loss that funds the gain of the group reached first. In the essay's "benefits held" pole the later cohort pays more; that is a sequence in birth order, whereas Mises's is a sequence in the order money is received. Same shape, different mechanism.
- Mises's point that only *unforeseen* change redistributes (II.12.14) has an analogue in the essay's forecasting claim: a demographic shift that is "locked in" decades ahead is foreseen, so on Mises's own logic it should already be in contract terms where contracts are free. Pension rules are set by statute, not by contract, which is where the analogy stops.
- II.12.16 makes Mises a sceptic of indexation, in 1912 as in 1924. Indexed pensions and inflation-linked bonds are routine today (background knowledge, not sourced in this session), so the essay should present this as his position, not as an established result.

### Gaps
- No passage in chs 1–14 discusses pensions, annuities, life insurance, widows, orphans, old age or retirement. The familiar "widows and orphans" theme is not in this book at all (see the grep section).
- Whether the exact wording of the gold-mine paragraph (II.12.33) and of the civil-servant sentences (II.12.36) is 1912 or 1924 was not determined; the argument is 1912, but Mises says he "in part revised" this chapter in 1924 (HP.22) and Hülsmann's collation does not cite these two paragraphs to "A" pages.
- Mises gives no magnitudes anywhere in ch. 12 (no estimate of the size of transfers), apart from the stylised 1/5 and 1/2 examples in II.13.45.

## How does chapter 13 (Monetary Policy) define inflationism, and what reasons does it give for governments resorting to it (inability to tax or borrow, war, concealment of costs)? What limits does he say inflation runs into?

### Takeaway
Inflationism is "that monetary policy that seeks to increase the quantity of money"; governments turn to it when they will not tax and cannot borrow, above all in war and under loss-making state enterprise, because it hides who pays. Its limits are that expected inflation stops helping debtors, its trade effects need ever-faster depreciation, its dose cannot be measured, and if continued it ends in flight from the currency.

- Three kinds of inflationist: the naive, who think money is wealth (II.13.9); those who want depreciation for its effects (II.13.10); and the "lesser evil" school (II.13.13) — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)

**Why governments inflate**

- Historically the motive was fiscal — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17); [ch.4](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=8)

  > [I.4.24 | ch.4 §3] In the great majority of cases, the state has taken such measures merely with certain fiscal ends in view. It has aimed to increase its own resources by the creation of credit money.
  > [II.13.37 | ch.13 §4] The existence and popularity of inflationism is due to the circumstance that it taps new sources of public revenue. Governments had inflated from fiscal motives long before it occurred to anybody to justify their procedure from the point of view of monetary policy.
- It is easy — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.7 | ch.13 §2] It is true that the means is extremely crude, and that the extent of its consequences can never be foreseen. But it is easy to apply and popular on account of its drastic effects.
- "Inflation is a kind of taxation" is an argument he *reports* others making; his own gloss is the unwilling-to-tax, unable-to-borrow formula — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.14 | ch.13 §3] Sometimes this sort of conditional inflation is supported by the argument that inflation is a kind of taxation that is advisable in certain circumstances.
  > [II.13.14 | ch.13 §3] The assistance of inflation is invoked whenever a government is unwilling to increase taxation or unable to raise a loan; that is the truth of the matter
- Why taxes will not do: taxpayers must assent to the purpose — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.15 | ch.13 §3] It is only possible to levy high taxes when those who bear the burden of the taxes assent to the purpose for which the resources so raised are to be expended.
- War finance — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.16 | ch.13 §3] Who has any doubt that the belligerent peoples of Europe would have tired of war much more quickly if their governments had clearly and candidly laid before them at the time the account of their war expenditure?
  > [II.13.16 | ch.13 §3] Even in England, the classical country of "sound money," the printing presses were set in motion.
- Concealment: false prosperity, taxed pseudo-profits, scapegoats — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.16 | ch.13 §3] Inflation had the great advantage of evoking an appearance of economic prosperity and of increase of wealth, of falsifying calculations made in terms of money, and so of concealing the consumption of capital.
  > [II.13.16 | ch.13 §3] Inflation made it possible to divert the fury of the people to "speculators" and "profiteers."
- After the war: subsidised bread, loss-making railways, the unemployed, reparations, the Ruhr (II.13.14, II.13.17) — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.17 | ch.13 §3] And the German and Austrian people would have been quicker in realizing where the resources came from that made bread cheaper if they themselves had to supply them in the form of a bread tax.

  > [II.13.18 | ch.13 §3] A government always finds itself obliged to resort to inflationary measures when it cannot negotiate loans and dare not levy taxes, because it has reason to fear that it will forfeit approval of the policy it is following if it reveals too soon the financial and general economic consequences of that policy.
  > [II.13.18 | ch.13 §3] Thus inflation becomes the most important psychological resource of any economic policy whose consequences have to be concealed; and so in this sense it can be called an instrument of unpopular, that is, of antidemocratic, policy, since by misleading public opinion it makes possible the continued existence of a system of government that would have no hope of the consent of the people if the circumstances were clearly laid before them. That is the political function of inflation.
  > [II.13.18 | ch.13 §3] When governments do not think it necessary to accommodate their expenditure to their revenue and arrogate to themselves the right of making up the deficit by issuing notes, their ideology is merely a disguised absolutism.
- Why it stays popular — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.35 | ch.13 §3] The favor it enjoys is due solely to the circumstance that it is a policy concerning whose aims and intentions public opinion can be longest deceived.

  > [II.13.39 | ch.13 §4] Now policies favoring creditors at the expense of debtors have never been popular. Lenders of money have been held in odium, at all times and among all peoples.

**Limits**

- Limit 1, anticipation: once expected, inflation is priced into interest and no longer favours debtors — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.11 | ch.13 §3] Depreciation of money can benefit debtors only when it is unforeseen.
  > [II.13.19 | ch.13 §3] A policy aiming at a progressive diminution of the value of money does not benefit debtors.
- Limit 2, the stimulus needs acceleration: effects last only while adjustment is incomplete — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.20 | ch.13 §3] The "beneficial effects" on trade of the depreciation of money only last so long as the depreciation has not affected all commodities and services. Once the adjustment is completed, then these "beneficial effects" disappear.
  > [II.13.20 | ch.13 §3] only the progressive diminution of the value of money could permanently achieve the aims which they have in view. But a monetary system that corresponds to these requirements can never be actually realized.
- Limit 3, knowledge: a steady fall of 1% a year is imaginable (II.13.21) but cannot be engineered — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.21 | ch.13 §3] We could quite easily imagine a monetary system in which the value of money was constantly falling at the same proportionate rate.
  > [II.13.22 | ch.13 §3] For we lack the most important prerequisite for this; we do not so much as know the quantitative significance of variations in the quantity of money.
  > [II.13.22 | ch.13 §3] In employing any means to influence the value of money we run the risk of giving the wrong dose.
- Limit 4, the public adapts: people switch their reckoning — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.23 | ch.13 §3] In proportion as the Germans began to reckon in terms of gold, so was further depreciation rendered incapable of altering the relationship between creditor and debtor or even of influencing trade.
- Limit 5, politics of excess — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.24 | ch.13 §3] A danger necessarily involved in all attempts to carry out an inflationary policy is that of excess.
- Limit 6, collapse and flight from money. Note his own concession in the same paragraph that "an endless process of depreciation can be imagined" — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.25 | ch.13 §3] Consistently and uninterruptedly continued inflation must eventually lead to collapse.
  > [II.13.26 | ch.13 §3] In the long run, a money which continually fell in value would have no commercial utility. It could not be used as a standard of deferred payments.
  > [II.13.26 | ch.13 §3] Everybody attempts to minimize his cash reserves, which are a source of continual loss.
- In rapid inflations value falls faster than quantity rises (his mP > Mp), because demand for the money falls — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.27 | ch.13 §3] In all countries where inflation has been rapid, it has been observed that the decrease in the value of the money has occurred faster than the increase in its quantity.
- Collapse destroys the state's paper, not money as such (United States 1781, France 1796) — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.31 | ch.13 §3] The collapse of an inflation policy carried to its extreme--as in the United States in 1781 and in France in 1796--does not destroy the monetary system, but only the credit money or fiat money of the state that has overestimated the effectiveness of its own policy.
- His three conclusions, and his refusal to judge the aims — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.32 | ch.13 §3] It is not the business of science to criticize the political aims of inflationism.
  > [II.13.33 | ch.13 §3] In the first place, all the aims of inflationism can be secured by other sorts of intervention in economic affairs, and secured better, and without undesirable incidental effects.
  > [II.13.34 | ch.13 §3] Second, there is no kind of inflationary policy the extent of whose effects can be foreseen. And finally, continued inflation must lead to a collapse.
- The same knowledge limit applies to *stabilising* the value of money, which is why he settles for metal — [ch.13](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=17)
  > [II.13.51 | ch.13 §5] Once the principle is so much as admitted that the state may and should influence the value of money, even if it were only to guarantee the stability of its value, the danger of mistakes and excesses immediately arises again.
  > [II.13.54 | ch.13 §6] All monetary policies encounter the difficulty that the effects of any measures taken in order to influence the fluctuations of the objective exchange value of money can neither be foreseen in advance, nor their nature and magnitude be determined even after they have already occurred.
- And gold is second best, not ideal (1934) — [1934/1924 prefaces](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=4)
  > [HP.12 | 1934 preface] Gold is not an ideal basis for a monetary system.

### Inferences
- "A tax nobody votes for" is a fair gloss of II.13.15–18 (taxes need assent; inflation is used when assent is lacking; it is "antidemocratic" policy) but it is not Mises's phrase. The exact words available are "the political function of inflation" and "an instrument of unpopular, that is, of antidemocratic, policy". He attributes "inflation is a kind of taxation" to inflation's defenders.
- The fiscal logic transfers to an ageing state by inference only: a government facing pension and health promises it will not fund by taxes or loans has, on II.13.18, the motive Mises describes. He names war, cheap food, state railways, unemployment relief and reparations as the expenditures; he never names pensions.
- Limit 3 is where later experience cuts against him. He says a regime of steady, small, known depreciation "can never be actually realized" (II.13.20–22). Inflation-targeting central banks have aimed at roughly that for decades (background knowledge; no source or figures were collected on this in this session); the essay should state this as counter-evidence if it uses the passage.
- The political-economy argument the essay most wants (governments inflate because they dare not tax) is Mises in 1924 looking back at 1914–23, not Mises in 1912 looking forward. The 1912 chapter argued from nineteenth-century history and, on Hülsmann's reading, hardly discussed motives.

## What does Mises say about the uneven, step-by-step way new money enters the economy and why changes in the quantity of money are never neutral or proportional?

### Takeaway
In ch. 8 §8 and ch. 12 §3 Mises argues that new money always reaches particular people first, raises the prices of what they buy first, and spreads with diminishing force; the final price structure differs from the starting one, so a doubling of money never simply halves its value. If money did change for everyone at once and in proportion, he says, nothing real would change at all.

### Cited Findings
- New money always starts in someone's cash balance — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.70 | ch.8 §8] An increase in a community's stock of money always means an increase in the amount of money held by a number of economic agents, whether these are the issuers of fiat or credit money or the producers of the substance of which commodity money is made.
- It spreads from their purchases outward — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.71 | ch.8 §8] Thus the increase of prices continues, having a diminishing effect, until all commodities, some to a greater and some to a lesser extent, are reached by it.
- The last receivers lose income — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.72 | ch.8 §8] The increase in the quantity of money does not mean an increase of income for all individuals. On the contrary, those sections of the community that are the last to be reached by the additional quantity of money have their incomes reduced, as a consequence of the decrease in the value of money called forth by the increase in its quantity; this will be referred to later.
- Relative prices are permanently changed — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.73 | ch.8 §8] Since the increased quantity of money is received in the first place by a limited number of economic agents only and not by all, the increase of prices at first embraces only those goods that are demanded by these persons; further, it affects these goods more than it afterward affects any others.
  > [II.8.73 | ch.8 §8] But there will not be such a complete adjustment of the increases that all prices increase in the same proportion.
- Against proportionality (the whole of paragraph II.8.75) — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.75 | ch.8 §8] There is no justification whatever for the widespread belief that variations in the quantity of money must lead to inversely proportionate variations in the objective exchange value of money, so that, for example, a doubling of the quantity of money must lead to a halving of the purchasing power of money.
- Even an equal-proportional gift to everyone (Hume's five gold pieces overnight; Mill's "another pound, shilling, or penny"; Fisher and Brown's extra dollar per dollar) would not raise prices uniformly, because people value the extra units differently (II.8.74, II.8.76, II.8.83) — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.74 | ch.8 §8] For, even in this quite impossible case, every increase in the quantity of money would necessarily cause an alteration in the conditions of demand, which would lead to a disparate increase in the prices of the individual economic goods.
- Comparative statics is granted; the path is what he denies — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.85 | ch.8 §8] If we compare two static economic systems, which differ in no way from one another except that in one there is twice as much money as in the other, it appears that the purchasing power of the monetary unit in the one system must be equal to half that of the monetary unit in the other.
  > [II.8.85 | ch.8 §8] All those who ascribe to variations in the quantity of money an inverse proportionate effect on the value of the monetary unit are applying to dynamic conditions a method of analysis that is only suitable for static conditions.
- He does not reject the quantity theory, only its mechanical form — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)
  > [II.8.48 | ch.8 §6] The core of the doctrine consists in the proposition that the supply of money and the demand for it both affect its value.
- The neutrality counterfactual, stated outright in ch. 12 — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.29 | ch.12 §3] If the objective exchange value of all the stocks of money in the world could be instantaneously and in equal proportion increased or decreased, if all at once the money prices of all goods and services could rise or fall uniformly, the relative wealth of individual economic agents would not be affected.
  > [II.12.29 | ch.12 §3] The variation in the value of money would have no other significance than that of a variation of the calendar or of weights and measures.
- Why it never happens, and why that alone creates the social effects — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.30 | ch.12 §3] The social displacements that occur as consequences of variations in the value of money result solely from the circumstance that this assumption never holds good.
  > [II.12.30 | ch.12 §3] And this alone is why such variations have an effect on the social distribution of income.
- The effects depend on the path, not the cause — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)
  > [II.12.36 | ch.12 §3] For the economic consequences of variations in the value of money are determined, not by their causes, but by the nature of their slow progress, from person to person, from class to class, and from country to country.
- Who pays — [ch.12](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=16)

### Inferences
- "Cantillon effect" is a later label. Mises does not use it in chs 1–14, and "neutral" appears there only in a legal sense ("economically neutral", I.4.8). The essay should say "what is now called a Cantillon effect" if it uses the term.
- His claim is strong: non-neutrality follows from sequence alone, with no appeal to sticky wages or contracts. Sticky incomes (labourers, civil servants) are his examples of who is late, not his reason why money is non-neutral.
- For the essay's "contributions held" pole, the nearest Misesian idea is that position in a sequence, not effort or merit, determines who bears the cost. That is a structural parallel only.

### Gaps
- Mises offers no evidence on how long the spread takes or how large the permanent relative-price changes are; II.8.73 says only that adjustment is incomplete.
- Modern empirical work on distributional effects of monetary expansion was not reviewed here.

## What do chapters 9, 10 and 14 say about exchange rates between currencies, purchasing-power parity and the balance-of-payments doctrine?

### Takeaway
Ch. 9 says money buys the same everywhere once goods in different places are treated as different goods; ch. 10 says two currencies exchange at the ratio of their purchasing powers (the "static or natural" ratio) and that money flows cause, not follow, trade balances; ch. 14 (new in 1924) uses the name "purchasing-power parity" and argues that a falling exchange rate is caused by inflation, never by an "unfavourable" balance of payments.

### Inferences
- Mises holds an absolute, tautology-leaning version of parity: any observed price gap is redefined as a difference in the good (its location), so the claim in II.9.11 cannot be refuted by price-level comparisons. The essay uses PPP-adjusted comparisons across countries; it should not cite Mises as evidence that PPP holds empirically. The finding that price levels are higher in richer countries (background knowledge, not sourced here) sits awkwardly with II.9.9 unless one accepts his redefinition. No empirical PPP literature was reviewed in this session.
- For the essay's section on current accounts: Mises's claim that the balance of trade "must always be in equilibrium" (II.10.11) refers to the full balance, including securities (II.14.24) and "present goods for future goods" (II.10.11). A persistent trade deficit financed by selling claims is therefore consistent with his view; he gives no theory of why a country's saving, and hence its capital flows, would change with its age structure.
- Mises's denial that "indebtedness to foreign nations" or "unfavorableness of the conditions of production" drives the exchange rate (II.14.25) is directed at nominal exchange rates under inflation. It says nothing about real exchange rates or real interest rates, which is where the ageing literature cited in the essay operates.

### Gaps
- Chs 9, 10 and 14 contain no data. The only numerical illustrations are hypothetical (gold/silver at 15½; cloth and wheat) or anecdotal (mark at ten centimes and Polish mark at five centimes in Zurich, II.14.9).
- The 1912 wording of II.10.4 could not be checked against the German first edition. HP.22 implies the "static" vocabulary ("static or natural ratio") is 1924, and the Cassel footnote cites a 1922 book.

### Inferences
- Any sentence in the essay that has Mises speaking about pensioners, widows, orphans or the old must be marked as the essay's inference. In Parts I–II his losers from inflation are creditors, savers in savings banks and bonds, late-adjusting wage earners and civil servants, and farmers in the gold-mine model.
- Population enters his monetary theory in chs 1–14 in exactly one way: a larger population (with more division of labour and prosperity) demands more money, which tends to raise money's value. He draws no conclusion about age structure. The symmetric statement about a *falling* population is in Part III (III.17.7), outside this dossier.
- "Posterity" (II.13.12) is the only intergenerational word in chs 1–14, and it appears in a concession: redistribution toward the rich might raise saving and so benefit later generations.

## For each argument, distinguish 1912/1924 text from anything later, and note where Mises qualifies or limits his own claim.

### Takeaway
Everything in chs 1–14 as printed is the 1924 second German edition in Batson's 1934 translation; nothing in those chapters dates from 1953, and only the prefaces are later (1934, 1952). Hülsmann's 2012 collation of the 1912 and 1924 editions settles the main cases: the welfare thesis (I.5.18), non-proportionality, early-versus-late redistribution and purchasing-power parity are 1912; the anticipation argument (II.12.13–14, II.13.11–12), the whole inflationism section with its fiscal and political motives (II.13.8–35), the index-number concession (ch. 11 §5) and ch. 14 are 1924.

- More money adds nothing: except where paper replaces costly commodity money (II.8.69), and except indirectly through redistribution toward savers (II.12.32, II.13.12) — [ch.8](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mises/msT.html?chapter_num=12)

### Inferences
- A dating formula the essay can defend: "1912" for the welfare sentence (I.5.18); "1912, revised 1924" for non-proportionality, early-versus-late redistribution and parity (chs 8, 9, 10, 12 §3); "1924" for anything about anticipated inflation and interest (II.12.13–14, II.13.11–12, II.13.19), for the whole inflationism section including "the political function of inflation" (II.13.18), for the savers and savings-bank sentences, and for ch. 14. Calling the "political function of inflation" passage a 1912 prediction would be wrong: it was written in 1924 about 1914–23.
- The two halves of the essay's likely argument therefore have different vintages. "Money is not wealth, and new money redistributes" is the pre-war theorist. "Governments inflate because they dare not tax, and savers pay" is the post-hyperinflation Mises. A sentence that runs them together under "in 1912 Mises argued" would misdate the second half.
- The qualifications matter for grading by the essay's own standard. Mises's strongest sentences (I.5.18; II.13.25) come with stated exceptions in the same or adjacent paragraphs, and the essay's rule that "counter-evidence [be] stated plainly" is met by quoting those exceptions from Mises himself.
